    Sadie Hawkins Day is observed on November 13 in the United States, and it began as a running gag in a comic strip. Al Capp introduced it in Li'l Abner in 1937 as an annual event in the invented town of Dogpatch, where the unmarried women were licensed for one day to chase down the bachelors, and the Library of Congress — which holds Capp's original drawings — says it "quickly became a national institution." What it did not have, for most of its life, was a fixed date.

    History

    Li'l Abner first appeared in 1934, in the New York Mirror, and ran until Capp retired in 1977. Capp set it in Dogpatch, a fictional Appalachian backwater, and Britannica describes the strip's annual Sadie Hawkins Day as the day on which "the women of the town were allowed to marry any bachelor they could chase down and capture." The Library of Congress dates Capp's introduction of the device to 1937 and records that he then repeated it every November, with Abner escaping matrimony each year until 1952, when Capp finally married his two leads off.

    The date moved. A Capp original held by the Library of Congress's Prints and Photographs Division, published on September 25, 1943 and carrying a United Feature Syndicate copyright line, is catalogued under the warning it contains: "Sadie Hawkins Day comes on Nov. 6th." Nothing in an acceptable source explains how November 13 became the conventional modern date, and nothing confirms the widely repeated claim that the first Sadie Hawkins strip ran on November 15, 1937. Both are worth treating as folklore until somebody produces the strip.

    Off the page it turned into a dance. Britannica notes that a day of role reversal, with women asking men, "was widely adopted at college campuses throughout the United States." American University's library archives put its own first Sadie Hawkins Dance in November 1939, two years after the strip, and record what the convention looked like on the ground: students dressed "Dogpatch style," vegetable corsages instead of flowers, and mock weddings in which couples exchanged pipe cleaner rings and collected wedding certificates.

    Timeline

    • 1934 — Li'l Abner first appears, in the New York Mirror.
    • 1937 — Al Capp introduces Sadie Hawkins Day in the strip; the Library of Congress records that he then repeated it every November.
    • November 1939 — American University students hold their first Sadie Hawkins Dance.
    • September 25, 1943 — A Capp original now in the Library of Congress warns Dogpatch bachelors that "Sadie Hawkins Day comes on Nov. 6th."
    • 1952 — Capp marries his two main characters, ending Abner's annual escape.
    • 1977 — Li'l Abner ends on Capp's retirement; he dies in 1979.

    How to observe

    Look at an original. The Library of Congress's Prints and Photographs Division holds Capp's drawings with their syndicate copyright lines and publication dates intact, and the catalog is searchable online. The 1943 "Nov. 6th" strip is the single best piece of evidence that the date was never fixed, and it takes about a minute to find.

    Check a campus archive rather than a holiday list. American University's archives follow its Sadie Hawkins dances from 1939 into the 1960s: annual through the 1940s and 1950s, moved to the spring from 1955, expanded into a Sadie Hawkins Weekend by 1963, with a Li'l Abner election in 1966 and 1967. Most American colleges with a digitized yearbook or student newspaper have a comparable run, and those records show what people actually did rather than what the day is supposed to mean.

    If a school near you still holds one, the name has now outlived its premise by close to ninety years, which is the genuinely interesting part of the tradition.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Sadie Hawkins Day always November 13?

    Not historically. The date was not fixed in the comic strip that created it: a 1943 Capp original held by the Library of Congress announces it for November 6. No acceptable source explains how November 13 became the usual modern date.

    Was Sadie Hawkins a real person?

    No. She was a character in Al Capp's Li'l Abner, set in the fictional town of Dogpatch. Britannica describes the day as one on which Dogpatch's women could marry any bachelor they could chase down and capture.

    When did the first Sadie Hawkins strip run?

    In 1937, according to the Library of Congress and American University's archives. The specific date of November 15, 1937, is repeated everywhere but appears in no source worth citing.

    When did Sadie Hawkins dances start?

    The earliest well-documented example this page could source is American University's first Sadie Hawkins Dance in November 1939, two years after the strip. Britannica says the role-reversal dance was widely adopted on American campuses, without giving a date.

    Is Li'l Abner still running?

    No. The strip ran from 1934 until 1977, when Capp retired. He died on November 5, 1979.
