    Kwanzaa runs for seven days, from December 26 through January 1, and is an African American and Pan-African holiday organized around seven principles rather than a single event. Maulana Karenga created it in 1966, which makes it unusual among holidays: its founder is living, has published a book on it, and maintains an authoritative account of what it is. One thing it is not, on his own insistence, is a substitute for Christmas.

    History

    Karenga created Kwanzaa in 1966 out of Kawaida, a cultural nationalist philosophy holding that the central challenge in Black life is the challenge of culture — that what is needed is to bring forth the best of that culture, ancient and current, and build from it. By his own account the holiday was made "in the midst of our struggles for liberation" and formed part of the work of his organization, Us, "to create, recreate and circulate African culture as an aid to building community, enriching Black consciousness, and reaffirming the value of cultural grounding for life and struggle." The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture describes the same founding more plainly, as a response to cultural disconnection among African Americans.

    The name comes from the Swahili phrase matunda ya kwanza, "first fruits," and the holiday is modeled on African first-fruits harvest celebrations — Karenga names the seven-day Umkhosi of Zululand in particular as a reason for its length. The larger reason is the Nguzo Saba, the seven principles, one per day: Umoja (unity), Kujichagulia (self-determination), Ujima (collective work and responsibility), Ujamaa (cooperative economics), Nia (purpose), Kuumba (creativity), and Imani (faith). Swahili is used because it is a Pan-African language rather than the language of one nation or group. Even the spelling carries its own history: kwanza has six letters, and in the early days of Us there were seven children who each wanted one, so an extra "a" was added.

    Karenga is direct about two misreadings. Kwanzaa is a cultural choice, not a religious one, and so not an alternative to anyone's faith. And he draws a sharp line between Ujamaa, the cooperative economics of artists and vendors supplying Kwanzaa materials, and corporate attempts to "penetrate and dominate the community Kwanzaa market" by borrowing the holiday's language to sell things.

    Timeline

    • 1966 — Maulana Karenga creates Kwanzaa, out of the Kawaida philosophy and the work of his organization Us.
    • 1997 — The U.S. Postal Service issues the first Kwanzaa commemorative stamp on October 22, from a painting by Synthia Saint James commissioned the year before.
    • 2004, 2009, 2011, 2013 — The Postal Service issues four further Kwanzaa stamp designs.
    • 2008 — Karenga publishes Kwanzaa: A Celebration of Family, Community and Culture (University of Sankore Press), the book his official site summarizes.
    • 2016 — A Kwanzaa Forever stamp is dedicated at Charleston's MOJA Arts Festival; December 26 of that year marks the holiday's fiftieth anniversary.

    How to observe

    Kwanzaa has a set form, and the founder's site is blunt that without definite guidelines and core values there is no holiday. A Kwanzaa Set is arranged in a central place at home: African cloth, then the mkeka (mat), then the kinara (candle holder) holding the mishumaa saba, the seven candles — one black, three red, three green, for the people, their struggle, and the hope that comes from the struggle. The black candle, Umoja, stands at the center and is lit first; the rest follow left to right over the week, so that the people come first, then the struggle, then the hope.

    The greeting is "Habari gani?" and the answer is the day's principle: Umoja on the 26th, Kujichagulia on the 27th, and so on through Imani.

    Gifts go mainly to children and should always include a book and a heritage symbol — the book for the African value of learning, the symbol to reaffirm tradition. On December 31, the sixth day, comes Karamu, a large feast. Karenga asks that Kwanzaa's symbols not be mixed with those of another culture, which he frames as a matter of Kujichagulia.

    Frequently asked questions

    What are the seven dates of Kwanzaa?

    December 26 through January 1, every year. The dates are fixed, so unlike Easter or Thanksgiving there is nothing to look up.

    Is Kwanzaa a religious holiday?

    No. Karenga is explicit that it is a cultural choice rather than a religious one, and that nobody has to give up a religion to practice a culture. The Postal Service and the Smithsonian both describe it as non-religious.

    Is Kwanzaa meant to replace Christmas?

    No, and the founder says so directly: Kwanzaa "is not a reaction or substitute for anything." Some celebrants treat it as an alternative to the commercial side of other holidays, but Karenga's position is that this is not its function. Many families keep both.

    Why is there an extra "a" in Kwanzaa?

    The Swahili word kwanza has six letters. In the early days of the Us organization seven children each wanted to represent a letter, so a seventh was added.

    Can people who are not of African descent take part?

    Karenga's answer is yes, with a distinction: Kwanzaa is an African holiday created for African peoples, and while public celebrations — at a school or college, say — can include others, the ritual itself should be conducted by the community whose holiday it is. As he puts it, audience attendance is one thing and conducting a ritual another.
