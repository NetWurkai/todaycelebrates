    April Fools' Day falls on April 1 and is kept across much of the Western world with no agreed account of where it came from. Asked the origin question directly, a Library of Congress folklorist answered: "The short answer is that nobody knows for sure." The uncertainty is not a modern failing — an almanac verse printed in 1760 already conceded that the people keeping the day could not say why.

    History

    The first certain reference comes from a 1561 Flemish poem by Eduard De Dene, in which a nobleman sends his servant on fruitless errands and the servant realizes he is being sent on fool's errands because it is April 1. The first English mention is John Aubrey's, in 1686, recording "Fooles Holy Day": "We observe it on the first of April... And so it is kept in Germany everywhere." The first description of an actual prank is dated April 2, 1698, when Dawk's News-Letter reported that "several persons were sent to the Tower Ditch to see the Lions washed" — the Tower of London's moat.

    The familiar origin stories do not survive contact with the sources. The Library of Congress classes the 1582 Gregorian calendar story as a migratory legend, told in variants that cannot all be true at once, and points to evidence that French books were already starting the year on January 1 by 1507, before the change that supposedly caused the custom. The Roman festival Hilaria is rejected in so many words: "there's no hard evidence to connect Hilaria with April Fools' Day." And the story that the day began with the emperor Constantine and a jester named Kugel was itself an April Fools' prank, played on an Associated Press reporter by a Boston University professor in 1983 and later retracted by the wire.

    Timeline

    • 1561 — The first certain reference to April Fools' Day: a Flemish poem by Eduard De Dene, whose servant realizes he is being sent on fool's errands because it is April 1.
    • 1686 — John Aubrey records the day in English for the first time, as "Fooles Holy Day," and notes it is kept in Germany everywhere.
    • April 2, 1698 — Dawk's News-Letter prints the first description of an April Fool's errand: "Several persons were sent to the Tower Ditch to see the Lions washed."
    • 1760 — Poor Robin's Almanac concedes the origin is already lost: "Nor I nor they themselves do know."
    • April 1, 1957 — The BBC broadcasts its Swiss spaghetti harvest report, among the first television April Fools' hoaxes.
    • April 1, 1996 — Taco Bell announces it has bought the Liberty Bell; public outcry follows and the company donates $50,000.

    How to observe

    The Library of Congress has done the useful work here. Its research guide April Fools' Day: Topics in Chronicling America picks out a dozen digitized newspaper articles running from 1871 to 1922, free to read. The best is the San Francisco Call of April 1, 1900, which opens with the Poor Robin's verse and then concedes that why people called the day so was already unclear in 1900. The guide gives its search strategy too, so you can run the same query against your own town's papers.

    For the day itself, NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day has a long April 1 spoof tradition and signs each one off as a joke; the 2002 entry had Hubble resolving an expiration date for the green cheese moon.

    Frequently asked questions

    Where did April Fools' Day come from?

    Nobody knows. A Library of Congress folklorist put it plainly: "The short answer is that nobody knows for sure." The earliest certain reference is the Flemish poem of 1561, which postdates every proposed origin.

    Is it true that April Fools' Day comes from the switch to the Gregorian calendar?

    There is no evidence for it. The Library of Congress treats the calendar story as a migratory legend told in versions that contradict one another, and cites a 1904 study showing French books were already beginning the year on January 1 as early as 1507 — before the change supposed to have caused the custom.

    Does April Fools' Day appear in Chaucer?

    Not reliably. The Nun's Priest's Tale, dated about 1390, contains the line "Syn March bigan, thritty dayes and two," which can be read as April 1. The Library of Congress notes that many scholars think "bigan" is a scribal error and the intended date was May 2, and says the passage "doesn't qualify as hard evidence."

    What was the BBC spaghetti harvest?

    On April 1, 1957, the BBC broadcast a report showing a family in Ticino, Switzerland harvesting spaghetti from trees. The narration began: "The last two weeks of March are an anxious time for the spaghetti farmer." Viewers called in to ask whether it was true and how to grow such a tree. CBC reports that the BBC considers it one of the first television April Fools' hoaxes.

    Has an April Fools' hoax ever had real consequences?

    Yes. In 1996 Taco Bell ran a full-page ad claiming it had bought the Liberty Bell and would rename it the Taco Liberty Bell. Angry callers flooded newspapers and the National Park Service, whose spokesperson said, "I believe I started hyperventilating." Taco Bell later donated $50,000. In 1998 two rock radio hosts were fired after telling listeners that the mayor of Boston had died in a car crash.
