    National Espresso Day falls on November 23. No founding organization, government page or news report designates it, and nothing on record explains the date — only calendar sites carry it. The drink is better documented than its holiday: the idea that made espresso espresso survives in a patent anyone can read.

    History

    No acceptable source says where National Espresso Day came from: no company, trade body or government page claims it. The drink's own history is documented.

    Britannica defines espresso as "a strong brew of coffee produced by forcing boiled water under pressure through finely ground coffee," notes that the Italian word carries the sense of "fast, express," and places the invention in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It begins with Angelo Moriondo, who patented in 1884 "new steam machinery for the economic and instantaneous confection of coffee beverage" — a prototype that still made coffee in bulk.

    The break came with Luigi Bezzera, and his argument survives in his own words. United States Patent 726,793, "Coffee-Making Machine," was applied for on June 10, 1902 under Serial No. 111,019 and issued on April 28, 1903 to "Luigi Bezzera, of Milan, Italy, assignor to Desiderio Pavoni, of Milan, Italy." The specification contrasts the machine with earlier appliances that prepared "a quantity of beverage corresponding to a certain number of cups, (fifty, for example,)" and then states the point: "the quantity of coffee necessary for a single cup is prepared separately, so that fresh coffee is always served." Britannica calls it the first known machine to force steam and hot water through ground coffee into an individual cup, cutting brewing time "from a few minutes to 30 seconds," and credits Pavoni with the pressure-release valve and the steam wand. The two showed the machine, the Ideale, at the 1906 World's Fair in Milan.

    One detail does not line up, and this page leaves it that way. Britannica says "Bezzera's 1902 patents were then purchased the following year by Desiderio Pavoni," while the patent itself, applied for in June 1902, already named Pavoni as assignee when it issued in April 1903.

    Crema — the "golden to reddish-brown foam" Britannica treats as a mark of a well-pulled shot because it "lingers on the surface for a minute or two" — is usually credited to Achille Gaggia, whom Britannica's espresso article never mentions. Gaggia's own company history says he filed patent no. 365726 on September 5, 1938 for a system called "Lampo" that produced "a soft layer of 'crema naturale'," later registered a lever-piston patent, and built a first machine, the Tipo Classica, with the entrepreneur Carlo Ernesto Valente. Those are the company's claims, and its page dates neither the lever patent nor the Classica. The independent record is later: US Patent 2,898,844, inventor printed as "Gaggia Giovanni Achille," assigned to Brevetti Gaggia SpA, priority March 23, 1955, granted August 11, 1959, claiming "a piston compressing water beneath said piston." It states no pressure and never uses the word crema.

    Timeline

    • 1884 — Angelo Moriondo patents "new steam machinery for the economic and instantaneous confection of coffee beverage," which still produces coffee only in bulk.
    • June 10, 1902 — Luigi Bezzera files his United States application, Serial No. 111,019.
    • April 28, 1903 — US Patent 726,793 issues to Bezzera of Milan, assignor to Desiderio Pavoni of Milan.
    • 1906 — Pavoni and Bezzera show the Ideale, and caffè espresso, at the World's Fair in Milan.
    • September 5, 1938 — Achille Gaggia files patent no. 365726 for "Lampo," which his company says produced a layer of "crema naturale."
    • August 11, 1959 — US Patent 2,898,844 is granted to Gaggia Giovanni Achille, assigned to Brevetti Gaggia SpA.

    How to observe

    Read the 1903 patent. It is short, it is free, and the sentence about preparing one cup at a time is the whole idea of espresso, written down before the word was famous.

    Do not assume a shot is a caffeine bomb. The FDA's consumer guidance gives 113 to 247 milligrams for a 12-fluid-ounce cup of regular brewed coffee and 400 milligrams a day as a ceiling for most adults — and publishes no espresso figure at all.

    Ask the bar what it is doing. Britannica lists the variables that separate two shots: gauge pressure, water temperature, dose and grind, seconds of extraction, and volume produced.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is National Espresso Day an official holiday?

    No. No government body, trade association or company designates it, and no acceptable source records who started it or why it falls on November 23.

    Who invented espresso?

    No single person. Britannica credits Angelo Moriondo with an 1884 bulk machine, Luigi Bezzera with the first machine to brew into an individual cup, and Desiderio Pavoni with the pressure-release valve and the steam wand.

    How much caffeine is in a shot of espresso?

    The FDA does not say. Its caffeine guidance gives 113 to 247 milligrams for 12 fluid ounces of brewed coffee and 400 milligrams a day as a general ceiling for most adults, but publishes no espresso figure, and notes that amounts vary by product and serving size.

    What is crema?

    Britannica describes it as the golden to reddish-brown foam on a shot, traditionally read as a sign of good preparation because it lingers for a minute or two instead of dissipating at once.

    Does espresso mean fast?

    Britannica gives "fast, express" for the Italian word, and credits Bezzera's machine with cutting brewing time to about 30 seconds.
