    The Great American Smokeout is the American Cancer Society's annual invitation to people who smoke to stop for one day, on the premise that a single fixed date is a plan someone can actually start on. It falls on the third Thursday of November, which in 2026 is November 19. The Society has run it nationally since 1977, a year after a California event persuaded close to a million people to quit for the day.

    History

    The Smokeout did not start at the American Cancer Society. By the Society's own account, the idea grew from a 1970 event in Randolph, Massachusetts, where Arthur P. Mullaney asked people to give up cigarettes for a day and donate what they would have spent to a high school scholarship fund. The money, not the medicine, was the hook.

    Four years later the idea surfaced in Minnesota, where in 1974 Lynn R. Smith, editor of the Monticello Times, spearheaded the state's first D-Day — Don't Smoke Day. Then, on November 18, 1976, the California Division of the American Cancer Society "got nearly 1 million people who smoke to quit for the day." The Society calls that the first official Smokeout and says it took the event nationwide in 1977.

    The backdrop matters. On January 11, 1964, Surgeon General Luther L. Terry released the first report of the Surgeon General's Advisory Committee on Smoking and Health, built on more than 7,000 articles, concluding that cigarette smoking caused lung and laryngeal cancer in men, probably caused lung cancer in women, and was the most important cause of chronic bronchitis. A decade on, when Randolph and Monticello were asking people to skip a day, smoking was still ordinary: 42.4% of American adults smoked in 1965, against 11.6% in 2022.

    The Society credits the event with helping shift American attitudes, and points to the state and local smoking bans, cigarette taxes and advertising limits that followed through the late 1980s and 1990s. Its framing of the stakes has not changed: cigarette smoking is the leading cause of cancer death in the United States, "accounting for 3 out of every 10 cancer deaths."

    The recent picture is less tidy than the long trend. Federal survey data show exclusive cigarette smoking among US adults falling from 10.8% in 2017 to 7.9% in 2023, roughly 6.8 million fewer people — but the CDC notes an offsetting increase of about 7.2 million adults using e-cigarettes exclusively, and that overall adult tobacco product use "has not changed since 2017."

    How to observe

    The Society's framing is the useful one: use the date to make a plan, or to start a cessation plan already made. The event is a day rather than a pledge because one day is a commitment people will actually make.

    If you are quitting, the Society is explicit that going alone is the hardest route. It names telephone quitlines, its Empowered to Quit email program, Nicotine Anonymous, self-help materials, counselors and support from friends and family, and says using two or more together works better than any one alone. Some people pair a prescription medicine with nicotine replacement; it suggests asking a doctor or pharmacist which fits. Its support line, 1-800-227-2345, runs 24 hours a day.

    If you do not smoke, the day is usually organized around someone who does. Workplaces, schools and hospitals build the date into cessation programs, and the Society publishes event tools for groups running one.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is the Great American Smokeout?

    The third Thursday of November — November 19 in 2026. The American Cancer Society has hosted it on that day for almost 50 years.

    Who started it, and when?

    The Society has run it nationally since 1977, but the idea came from outside: a 1970 event in Randolph, Massachusetts, Minnesota's D-Day in 1974, and a California Division event on November 18, 1976 that the Society counts as the first official Smokeout.

    Is quitting for a single day supposed to accomplish anything?

    The Society treats the date as a starting point rather than an end — a day to commit to a plan or begin one. It also says quitting usually takes more than one attempt, works best with support, and that combining two or more methods beats any one alone.

    What help is available?

    Telephone quitlines, the Society's Empowered to Quit email program, Nicotine Anonymous, self-help materials, counselors, support from family and friends, prescription medication and nicotine replacement. The Society's line is 1-800-227-2345, open 24 hours.

    Has American smoking actually gone down?

    Over the long run, yes: 42.4% of adults smoked in 1965 and 11.6% in 2022. The short run is murkier. Exclusive cigarette smoking fell from 10.8% of adults in 2017 to 7.9% in 2023, but the CDC reports overall adult tobacco product use unchanged since 2017, the drop offset by a rise in exclusive e-cigarette use.
