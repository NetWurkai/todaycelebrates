    National Parfait Day is marked on November 25, and nobody has ever claimed it: no dairy group, ice cream company, trade body or government page designates it, and the date appears only on calendar sites that name neither a founder nor a first year. The dessert's own history is documented, and confusing. It began as a custard frozen in a mold without churning, and in American soda fountains it became something else.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, proclamation or news report stands behind it, and nothing explains the November 25 date. What can be sourced is the dessert, and the dessert is two different things.

    The word entered English in 1894. Merriam-Webster gives the etymology as French, literally "something perfect," from parfait, perfect, from the Latin perfectus, and dates the first known use to the sense it still lists first: a flavored custard with whipped cream and syrup, frozen without stirring. That last clause is the distinction: a parfait in the older sense is not churned but mixed, poured into a mold and left to set.

    Escoffier's A Guide to Modern Cookery, the 1907 English edition of Le guide culinaire, places the parfait among the light ices molded and frozen directly rather than worked in a freezer. His generic recipe is thirty egg yolks beaten into a quart of cold syrup, cooked like an English cream, whisked over ice until cold, finished with whipped cream and a little brandy or rum, molded and packed in ice for two to three hours. He notes the term had formerly applied only to parfait au café. The same book carries a savory parfait de foies gras, which he advises buying ready-made because fresh foie gras travels badly — the sense a chicken liver parfait still follows.

    The American parfait is a different dish under the same name. The National Soda Fountain Guide, published in St. Louis in 1913 by William S. Adkins, calls it "a sort of first cousin to the sundae": ice cream for a base, the syrup mixed in rather than poured over, whipped cream worked through, nuts or crushed fruit added, served in a stemmed or narrow glass. Its chapter on the layer parfait reports that newer form had "recently become very popular," with formulas to match — a gingersnap parfait of five or even seven layers, built in a glass wide enough to take a whole biscuit, and a streaked strawberry parfait of thin ice cream layers alternating with single berries, which a Broadway drug store had sold the previous season. Merriam-Webster still lists both senses, and the yogurt-and-granola parfait sold at breakfast counters is the layered one.

    Timeline

    • 1894 — "Parfait" is first recorded in English, meaning a flavored custard with whipped cream and syrup, frozen without stirring.
    • 1907 — The English edition of Escoffier's Le guide culinaire gives a generic parfait: a cooked egg-and-syrup custard finished with whipped cream and brandy or rum, molded and frozen without churning. He notes the word had formerly meant only parfait au café.
    • 1907 — The same book's cold-dishes chapter carries a savory parfait de foies gras, to be bought ready-made rather than attempted.
    • c. 1912 — A Broadway drug store sells a "streaked strawberry parfait" of thin ice cream layers and single berries, reported the next year as the past season's success.
    • 1913 — The National Soda Fountain Guide calls the parfait a first cousin to the sundae and reports that the layer parfait has recently become very popular.

    How to observe

    Read the sources instead of a listicle: Escoffier's 1907 edition and Adkins's 1913 guide are both out of copyright and free to read in full. Then make one of the two. The 1907 frozen parfait needs no ice cream machine, which is the point of it: a cooked custard base, whipped cream, a mold and a few hours — though thirty egg yolks are hotel quantities and want scaling down. The 1913 layer parfait needs only ice cream, a tall glass and something with texture between the layers; try the gingersnap version first, because the biscuit softens into the ice cream instead of sitting in it.

    If you are ordering out instead, the word still points two ways: a parfait on a dessert menu is the frozen or layered sweet, and one on a starter menu is closer to Escoffier's foie gras version.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who created National Parfait Day?

    No one that can be sourced. No dairy or ice cream trade group, manufacturer, agency or news outlet claims the designation, and nothing explains the date. It appears on calendar aggregators, which name neither a founder nor a first year.

    What is the difference between a parfait and a sundae?

    In American soda-fountain terms, the syrup. The National Soda Fountain Guide of 1913 describes the parfait as a first cousin to the sundae, with the syrup mixed into the ice cream rather than poured over it, and served in a stemmed or narrow glass. In the older French sense the two are not comparable: a parfait there is a frozen custard.

    Is a yogurt parfait a real parfait?

    By the dictionary, yes. Merriam-Webster's second sense is a cold dessert of layers of fruit, syrup, ice cream and whipped cream, and the layered form has carried the name since at least 1913. It is simply not the 1894 sense, which requires a custard frozen without stirring.

    Why is a chicken liver parfait called a parfait?

    Because the word was never only a dessert term: Escoffier's 1907 guide includes a parfait de foies gras among the cold dishes, and the smooth molded savory preparation served in restaurants follows it.
