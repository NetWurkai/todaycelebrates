    New Year's Eve

    Send viaEmailTextFacebookXWhatsApp

    Recurs
    Every year on December 31

    Celebration

    New Year's Eve is December 31, and in the United States it holds two very different midnights. One is a commercial spectacle a newspaper owner invented in 1904 to publicize his new building. The other is a vigil that Black congregations have kept since the night of December 31, 1862, when people waited in the dark for the Emancipation Proclamation to take effect.

    History

    The Times Square version began as advertising, and the Times Square Alliance, which runs the celebration today, says so plainly. Adolph Ochs, the German Jewish immigrant who owned The New York Times, had persuaded the city to rename Longacre Square after his paper in 1904. That December he marked the opening of the Times Tower with an all-day street festival ending in fireworks shot from the base of the building, and more than 200,000 people came.

    The city banned the fireworks two years later. Ochs's replacement was a lighted ball lowered down the tower's flagpole at midnight to begin 1908, built by a young immigrant metalworker named Jacob Starr: iron and wood, five feet across, 700 pounds, lit by one hundred 25-watt bulbs. Starr's company, Artkraft Strauss, lowered it for most of the century that followed. The ball changed with its materials — wrought iron in 1920, aluminum in 1955, crystal in 2000, LEDs in 2007 — and the one unveiled in November 2025 is 12.5 feet across and weighs 12,350 pounds. It has descended every year since 1907 except 1942 and 1943, when New York's wartime dimout darkened the square; crowds gathered anyway and marked midnight with a minute of silence and chimes rung from sound trucks at the tower's base.

    The other American tradition is quieter and was built on far higher stakes. Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation on September 22, 1862, declaring enslaved people in the rebelling states free — but it would not take effect until the new year. So on the night of December 31, 1862, as the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture records, enslaved and free African Americans gathered, many of them in secret, to wait. The proclamation took effect at midnight, at the start of January 1, 1863, and the night became known as Watch Night, or Freedom's Eve.

    Gathering at all carried risk: Georgia's slave code of 1848 barred people of color from preaching or joining a religious meeting where more than seven were present. People worshipped anyway, in plantation praise houses and in "hush harbors" out in the woods, and the museum records the account of Charlotte Martin, a formerly enslaved woman in Florida whose brother was beaten to death for taking part. Watch Night services are still kept, generally beginning around 7 p.m. and running through midnight.

    Timeline

    • September 22, 1862 — Lincoln issues the Emancipation Proclamation, declaring enslaved people in the rebelling states free, to take effect with the new year.
    • December 31, 1862 — African Americans gather, many in secret, to await its effect; the night becomes known as Watch Night, or Freedom's Eve. The proclamation takes effect at midnight on January 1, 1863.
    • 1904 — Adolph Ochs marks the opening of the Times Tower with an all-day festival ending in fireworks; more than 200,000 people attend.

    How to observe

    Look for a Watch Night service. They are widely held and generally open, and the history above is why the room goes quiet in the minutes before midnight rather than loud.

    Do not plan the night around a federal day off: December 31 is not a federal holiday, and reaches that calendar only when January 1 falls on a Saturday. The next of those is 2028.

    Arrange the ride before you drink. NHTSA counted 11,904 deaths in drunk-driving crashes in 2024, about 32 a day. It publishes no New Year's Eve-specific figure, so treat the familiar "deadliest night of the year" line with suspicion.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is New Year's Eve a federal holiday?

    No. The Office of Personnel Management lists eleven legal public holidays under 5 U.S.C. 6103 and December 31 is not among them. It reaches the federal calendar only as the in-lieu-of date for New Year's Day when January 1 falls on a Saturday, as it does in 2028.

    What is Watch Night?

    A New Year's Eve service rooted in the night of December 31, 1862, when enslaved and free African Americans gathered, often in secret, to wait for the Emancipation Proclamation to take effect at midnight on January 1, 1863. Services are still held, generally beginning around 7 p.m. and running through midnight.

    Has the Times Square ball ever failed to drop?

    Twice, in 1942 and 1943, when New York's wartime dimout darkened the square. Crowds gathered anyway and marked midnight with a minute of silence and chimes rung from sound trucks at the base of the tower.

    Why did the ball drop start in the first place?

    Because the city banned the fireworks. Adolph Ochs's 1904 celebration for the opening of the Times Tower ended in a fireworks display, which was prohibited two years later, so to begin 1908 he had a lighted ball lowered down the tower's flagpole instead.
