    National Cashew Day falls on November 23. No founding body, government page or news report designates it; only calendar sites carry the name. The plant is the more interesting half: a Brazilian tree whose nut is a seed, whose apple is a swollen flower stalk, and whose shell is caustic enough that nobody sells cashews in it.

    History

    The observance has no sourceable origin. No grower group, processor, agriculture department or news outlet claims it.

    The tree does better. The University of Oxford's Plants 400 profile gives it as Anacardium occidentale L. (Anacardiaceae), "an evergreen tree endemic to regions of central and north-eastern Brazil with pronounced dry seasons." The order of growth is the part most people have backward: "a kidney-shaped, single-seeded fruit develops" first, and only afterward does the flower stalk swell into "a fleshy, brightly coloured, pear-shaped structure, commonly called a 'cashew apple'." The apple is not the fruit, and the nut is not a nut — "the familiar cashew nut is the plant's seed."

    The shell explains the supply chain. Oxford says cashews "are sold separated from the fruit, as the wall of the latter contains an irritant oil that may be highly allergenic," and names anacardic acid, cardol and cardanol among the chemicals drawn from it. India's Agricultural and Processed Food Products Export Development Authority, a government body, gives the industrial answer: "Cashew kernels are obtained through processing (roasting / steaming, shelling, and peeling) of raw Cashewnut."

    How a Brazilian tree became an Asian crop is documented on both ends. Oxford: "By the mid-sixteenth century, the Portuguese had moved the tree to India, from whence it was probably introduced to Africa and southeast Asia." APEDA says the same from the receiving side — cashew was "introduced to India in 16th century by Portuguese" and "initially introduced in Goa and further expanded to other states" — and lists the growing states along both Indian coasts.

    For American shoppers the consequential fact is regulatory. FDA's guidance for industry, Questions and Answers Regarding Food Allergens, Edition 5 (January 2025), carries a table of the tree nuts the agency treats as major food allergens, and the entry reads "Cashew Anacardium occidentale L. (Anacardiaceae)." The guidance adds that "Only the tree nuts listed in Table 1 are considered major food allergens." The authority runs back to the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act of 2004, which defined "major food allergen" in section 201(qq) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, and to the FASTER Act of 2021, which added sesame as the ninth.

    Scale is harder to state cleanly. Oxford puts 2018 global kernel production at "approximately 5.9 million tonnes, with 70% produced by three countries - Vietnam, India and Côte d'Ivoire," and says Brazil grew "nearly 90% of the 1.7 million tonnes of cashew apples" that year, naming no source for either number. APEDA reports India's output rising from 794,000 tonnes in 2023-24 to 802,000 tonnes in 2024-25, and ranks India fourth for exports of shelled cashews in 2024. Different years, different measures; both stand as each body states them.

    Timeline

    • Mid-16th century — the Portuguese move the tree out of Brazil to India, and from there probably to Africa and southeast Asia (University of Oxford).
    • 16th century — India's first plantings are in Goa, before the crop spreads to other states (APEDA).
    • August 2004 — the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act defines "major food allergen" in section 201(qq) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act.
    • 2018 — global cashew kernel production reaches roughly 5.9 million tonnes, 70 percent from Vietnam, India and Côte d'Ivoire (Oxford, which names no source).
    • April 2021 — the FASTER Act adds sesame as the ninth major food allergen.
    • January 2025 — FDA's Edition 5 guidance lists "Cashew Anacardium occidentale L. (Anacardiaceae)" among the tree nuts treated as major food allergens.

    How to observe

    Eat the half of the plant that does not travel. The cashew apple is perishable and is used where it grows, which is why Brazil accounts for almost all of them and why the juice is common there and rare elsewhere.

    Read a label on purpose. Because cashew sits in FDA's Table 1, a packaged food made with it must name it, either in parentheses in the ingredient list or in a "contains" statement.

    Do not try to shell a raw cashew. The oil in the shell wall is the reason an entire processing industry exists.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is a cashew a nut?

    Not botanically. Oxford's profile describes the cashew as the seed of a kidney-shaped, single-seeded fruit. It is a tree nut for labeling purposes, which is a regulatory category rather than a botanical one.

    Why can't you buy cashews in the shell?

    Because the shell wall holds what Oxford calls "an irritant oil that may be highly allergenic," containing anacardic acid, cardol and cardanol. APEDA describes the standard processing as roasting or steaming, shelling and peeling, all done before sale.

    What is the cashew apple?

    The swollen flower stalk, which grows after the fruit does. Oxford calls it "a fleshy, brightly coloured, pear-shaped structure." It is edible, perishable, and grown overwhelmingly in Brazil.

    Must cashews be declared on US food labels?

    Yes. FDA's Edition 5 guidance names cashew in its table of tree nuts treated as major food allergens.

    Is National Cashew Day an official holiday?

    No. It has no proclamation, no sponsoring organization and no documented origin.
