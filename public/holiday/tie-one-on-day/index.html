    Celebration

    Tie One On Day falls on the day before Thanksgiving, which puts it on November 25 in 2026, and despite the name it has nothing to do with drinking. The "one" is an apron. EllynAnne Geisel, a Colorado writer and apron collector, began the day after she wrapped a baked good in an apron, tucked in a note, and gave it to a neighbor who was having a hard time.

    History

    The day has a named founder, which makes it unusual among November observances. NPR reported in 2017 that Geisel started Tie One On Day after she saw what that gift meant to her neighbor, and that she set it on the day before Thanksgiving. Neither NPR nor her own site gives a founding year outright: NPR, writing in November 2017, places the original gift twelve years earlier, which points to 2005, while her site called 2017 the twelfth year of the initiative, which points to 2006. No source pins it down further.

    Geisel came to aprons sideways. After years as a full-time homemaker, she decided when her children left home to try writing, and took as her first subject the apron as the icon of her homemaking years. She began collecting aprons, linens, utensils, cookbooks and recipe boxes, and wrote of feeling a connection to earlier generations of women who had tied one on as "daily domestic armor." She was among the women who discarded their aprons in the 1960s, when, as the Associated Press put it, they had come to stand for household drudgery and women's oppression.

    The collection became an exhibit and a book. The AP reported in 2013 that Geisel, of Pueblo, Colorado, curates a traveling museum exhibit, Apron Chronicles, launched in 2004 with 150 vintage aprons and 46 stories and images. Her book, The Apron Book, was published by Andrews McMeel in 2006 — NPR ran a correction to confirm that year — and carries both the stories she had collected and patterns for three basic apron styles.

    One apron explains the enterprise better than the rest. A reader sent Geisel a black checkered chef-style apron with a dry cleaner's twill label still sewn to one tie. It had belonged to a famous cookbook author; his housekeeper's niece inherited it, did not cook, and decided it should go to someone who would care. The author was Craig Claiborne, whose New York Times Cookbook — the 1975 edition, a wedding present with a note saying that if she could read, she could cook — taught Geisel to cook in the first place.

    Timeline

    • 1960s — Aprons come to stand for household drudgery and women's oppression and are widely discarded; Geisel gives hers away.
    • 2004 — Apron Chronicles, a traveling exhibit of 150 vintage aprons with 46 stories and images, launches.
    • 2005 or 2006 — Geisel wraps a baked good in an apron for a neighbor in difficulty and starts Tie One On Day on the day before Thanksgiving; sources differ by a year and none states one outright.
    • 2006 — The Apron Book is published by Andrews McMeel, with the collected stories and patterns for three apron styles.
    • 2017 — NPR covers the day and the collection, and an updated edition of the book is reissued.

    How to observe

    The instructions on Geisel's own site are three steps: wrap a loaf of bread or another baked good in an apron, tuck an encouraging note into the pocket, and give it to a neighbor, a friend or someone in the community who could use the gesture. The pocket is the reason it is an apron and not a dish towel. She told the AP it is a chance to do an act of kindness before giving thanks, and that many people "need to know they are not invisible."

    If you have no spare apron, an apron is the standard first sewing project; a sewing instructor interviewed by the AP called it a good way to learn a machine, and gave the shortest version of all: attach a length of ribbon to a flour-sack towel, since the edges are already finished.

    If you own one you never wear, which is the common case with an inherited apron, Geisel's practice is to pass aprons on with their history attached, with a note telling the new owner who wore it before.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Tie One On Day?

    The day before Thanksgiving, which is November 25 in 2026. It is not a fixed date; it moves with Thanksgiving, and every source for the day describes it that way.

    Does Tie One On Day have anything to do with drinking?

    No. Merriam-Webster does list "tie one on" as slang for getting drunk, which is the name's joke, but the observance is about tying on an apron: an apron, a baked good and a note for a neighbor.

    Who started Tie One On Day?

    EllynAnne Geisel, a writer and apron collector in Pueblo, Colorado, who curates the traveling exhibit Apron Chronicles and wrote The Apron Book. NPR credits her with starting it after she gave an apron-wrapped baked good to a neighbor in hardship.

    What year did it start?

    No source says. NPR's 2017 account dates the original gift to twelve years earlier, which points to 2005, while Geisel's site called 2017 the twelfth year, which points to 2006. This page does not pick between them.

    What are you supposed to do?

    Wrap a loaf of bread or a baked good in an apron, put an encouraging note in the apron's pocket, and give it to someone who could use it — a neighbor, a friend, anyone having a hard time. That is the whole of it.
