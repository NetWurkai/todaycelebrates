    National Bread Pudding Day falls on November 13 and marks a dish that exists because bread goes stale. The observance itself has no traceable origin — no founding organization, proclamation or first year appears in any reliable source — but the dish it names is among the better-documented desserts in English and American cookbooks, printed and reprinted from the 1700s onward.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, proclamation or first year can be traced to a reliable source, which is the usual situation for single-dish food days.

    The dish is better served. The claim that circulates most widely — that bread pudding was invented by eleventh-century English cooks and known as a poor man's pudding — appears in no primary or scholarly source; the citations behind it lead to a personal blog and a user-generated site. What can be shown is that the dish was fully established in print by the eighteenth century. The Louisiana Endowment for the Humanities' 64 Parishes encyclopedia points to an early recipe in the 1742 edition of E. Smith's The Compleat Housewife, which opens by taking a penny loaf, cutting it in thin slices, and boiling a quart of cream or new milk.

    By the end of that century it was a category rather than a recipe. A 1796 edition of Hannah Glasse's The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy lists four of them in its contents: a fine bread pudding, an ordinary one, a baked one, and a bread and butter pudding. It crossed the Atlantic with the cookbooks. Amelia Simmons' American Cookery, printed at Hartford in 1796 and the first cookbook written by an American and published in America, gives "A Bread Pudding" beginning with one pound of soft bread or biscuit soaked in one quart of milk and run through a sieve. Mary Randolph's The Virginia House-Wife lists a bread pudding at page 124. By 1861 Isabella Beeton's Book of Household Management indexed baked, boiled and brown bread puddings alongside two kinds of bread-and-butter pudding — by then a small family of dishes rather than one.

    How to observe

    Use bread that is actually stale. 64 Parishes makes the point that bread puddings are often made with stale bread, and the reason is mechanical rather than sentimental: dry crumb takes up custard without collapsing into paste.

    Cook a version with a date attached to it. Simmons' 1796 recipe runs a few lines — soft bread or biscuit soaked in milk, pushed through a sieve, enriched and baked — and the full text is free to read online, as is the Glasse contents list that shows how many variants an eighteenth-century cook was expected to know.

    For the American regional version, New Orleans is where bread pudding stopped being thrift and became a restaurant dish. Lafcadio Hearn's La Cuisine Creole printed a "Delicious Bread Pudding" in 1885; the WPA's Louisiana Writers' Project later recorded an old local nickname for it, the "heavy devil"; and the city's chefs revived it from the 1970s, Paul Prudhomme's bread pudding soufflé for Commander's Palace's centennial among them.

    Frequently asked questions

    Did bread pudding really start in the eleventh century?

    No reliable source says so. The claim circulates widely, but the citations behind it lead to a personal blog and a user-generated site rather than to any primary or scholarly record. The earliest bread pudding recipes that can actually be pointed to are in eighteenth-century English cookbooks.

    What is the difference between bread pudding and bread and butter pudding?

    Cookbooks have treated them as related but separate for centuries. Glasse's 1796 contents list them as different recipes, and Beeton's 1861 index gives baked, boiled and brown bread puddings alongside baked and plain boiled bread-and-butter puddings.

    Why is bread pudding associated with New Orleans?

    The Louisiana Endowment for the Humanities' encyclopedia traces a long local line: Lafcadio Hearn's La Cuisine Creole printed a recipe in 1885, the Louisiana Writers' Project recorded the nickname "heavy devil," and city chefs revived the dish from the 1970s, including Paul Prudhomme's bread pudding soufflé made for Commander's Palace's centennial.

    Is bread pudding a dessert?

    Britannica defines a pudding as any of several foods sharing a relatively soft, spongy and thick texture, thickened with ingredients including bread, and notes that in Britain "pudding" serves as a generic term for sweet desserts.

    Who created National Bread Pudding Day?

    Not known. No founding organization, proclamation or first observance can be traced to a source worth citing.
