    National Coffee Day falls on September 29 in the United States, and what it mostly consists of is free coffee: chains and roasters use the date for giveaways and discounts. Nobody has documented who created it or why the date is September 29. The adjacent International Coffee Day, on October 1, does have a paper trail, and the contrast between the two is the most interesting thing about either.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, proclamation or recorded first observance meets this site's sourcing standard, and the National Coffee Association, the U.S. coffee trade association and the body most likely to have created it, makes no such claim anywhere on its site. The NCA marks the day publicly and repeatedly without ever saying where it came from: a September 2024 release described its polling as "published ahead of National Coffee Day on September 29," and on September 30, 2025 it reported briefing members of Congress on coffee's economic contribution as part of that year's observance. That is an organization marking a date it inherited, not one claiming to have set it.

    The international day is the instructive contrast. The International Coffee Organization's own page states that "in March 2014, the Member States of the ICO agreed to organize International Coffee Day on 1 October to create a single day of celebration for coffee lovers around the world," noting that many countries already kept their own coffee days. The widely repeated claim that the day was launched in Milan in 2015 appears nowhere on the ICO's pages, which date the decision to 2014 and name no launch location.

    What September 29 is actually for, on the evidence, is promotions. An ABC News owned-station report from September 29, 2021 listed free brewed coffee at Starbucks for customers who brought a clean reusable cup, a free medium coffee at Dunkin' for rewards members, and free coffee at Panera for parents and caregivers, with Dunkin' calling the date "the biggest coffee holiday of the year." Those offers expired in 2021; the shape of the day has not.

    The drink is better recorded than the holiday, though its beginning is not. Britannica traces wild coffee plants "probably from Kefa (Kaffa), Ethiopia," taken to southern Arabia and cultivated in the fifteenth century, and files the Kaldi story, the goatherd who around 850 CE "supposedly" tried the berries after watching his flock, as one of many legends. The drink went on to produce a social institution that had not existed before: the coffeehouse.

    Americans have not let up. The NCA's 2026 National Coffee Data Trends Fall Report, conducted in June 2026 among 1,882 adults, found past-day coffee consumption steady at 66 percent of American adults, more than any other beverage including tap or bottled water. Specialty coffee reached a record 48 percent, now ahead of traditional, and 83 percent of past-day drinkers have it at home.

    Timeline

    • About 850 CE — The traditional date of the Kaldi story, in which a goatherd notices his flock's behavior after it eats coffee berries. Britannica calls this one of many legends.
    • 15th century — Wild coffee plants, probably from Kefa (Kaffa) in Ethiopia, are taken to southern Arabia and placed under cultivation.
    • Through the 17th century — Nearly the entire world supply comes from Yemen, and by the century's close coffeehouses are flourishing across Britain, its American colonies and continental Europe.
    • March 2014 — The member states of the International Coffee Organization agree to organize International Coffee Day on October 1.
    • September 30, 2025 — The National Coffee Association marks National Coffee Day with a briefing for members of Congress on coffee's economic contribution.
    • September 15, 2026 — The NCA's Fall 2026 report puts past-day coffee consumption at 66 percent of American adults.

    How to observe

    Check the offers the day before rather than the morning of. Nearly all now require an app order, a loyalty account or a code, and the reusable-cup conditions are specific; the people who miss out arrive at the counter expecting a free cup.

    If you want the day to be about coffee rather than discounts, buy a single-origin bag from a roaster who names the farm or cooperative and the harvest year. That information is the entire argument of the ICO's October 1 day, which points at the people who grow the crop rather than the ones who drink it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who started National Coffee Day?

    Nobody credible has documented it. There is no founding organization, proclamation or recorded first observance this site can cite, and the National Coffee Association marks the day every year without ever claiming to have created it.

    Is National Coffee Day the same as International Coffee Day?

    No. National Coffee Day is September 29 in the United States. International Coffee Day is October 1, and it has a documented origin: the member states of the International Coffee Organization agreed to it in March 2014.

    Was International Coffee Day launched in Milan in 2015?

    That claim circulates widely but appears nowhere on the International Coffee Organization's own pages, which date the agreement to March 2014 and give no launch location. This site follows the ICO.

    How many Americans drink coffee every day?

    Sixty-six percent of American adults had coffee in the past day, per the National Coffee Association's Fall 2026 report, conducted in June 2026 among 1,882 adults, more than any other beverage including tap or bottled water. Its Spring 2025 report put the average at three cups a day.

    Is the free coffee real?

    Yes, but conditional. Most offers require a loyalty account, an app order or a specific action; Starbucks' 2021 giveaway required bringing a clean reusable cup. The deals change every year, so last year's list is not a guide.

    Omitted for lack of a source: any founder, founding year or first observance for the September 29 American date, and any reason for the choice of September 29. The NCA's silence on its own site is the strongest evidence available, and it is silence rather than a competing account.

    Source conflict, resolved toward the more primary source rather than averaged: the widely repeated claim that International Coffee Day was launched in Milan in 2015, against the ICO's own statement that its member states agreed the October 1 date in March 2014. The ICO is the body that created the day, so its account governs, and the page notes the discrepancy rather than hiding it. The two are not strictly incompatible, since an agreement in 2014 and a first celebration in 2015 could both be true, but this page will not assert a 2015 Milan launch that the founding organization's own pages do not mention.

    Figure dated rather than presented as current: the three-cups-a-day average, which comes from the NCA's Spring 2025 report that the NCA itself now flags as out of date. The 2026 report does not restate a cups-per-day number, so the page gives the figure with its date attached.

    Not checked, and therefore not claimed: whether any U.S. presidential proclamation or congressional resolution has ever recognized a National Coffee Day. No such record surfaced in the sources consulted and none of the coffee-industry sources references one, but congress.gov and the Federal Register were not searched. The page says the origin is undocumented rather than that no government record exists.
