    National French Dip Day marks a sandwich claimed by two Los Angeles restaurants that both opened in 1908 and have been arguing about it ever since. Philippe the Original dates the invention to 1918 and tells a story about how it happened; Cole's calls itself the originator and gives no date at all. Nothing available settles which is right — and the observance's own November 12 date has no traceable origin either.

    History

    A French dip is roast beef on a French roll, served with the juices from the roasting pan: either dipped before it reaches the table or poured into a cup alongside. Nothing about it is French, and the name is as undocumented as the invention.

    Philippe the Original tells the fuller story. Its own history says the restaurant "was established in 1908 by Philippe Mathieu, who claimed the distinction of having created the 'French Dipped Sandwich,'" and dates the moment to 1918: "One day in 1918, while making a sandwich, Mathieu inadvertently dropped the sliced french roll into the roasting pan filled with juice still hot from the oven." The customer, a policeman, "said he would take the sandwich anyway and returned the next day with some friends asking for more dipped sandwiches." On where the name came from, Philippe's is candid: "The answer is lost to history." Harry, Dave and Frank Martin bought the business from Mathieu in 1927 for around $5,000, and in 1951 the restaurant was pushed off Aliso Street to make room for the new Hollywood-Santa Ana freeway, moving to its present Alameda Street building — then a machine shop with a hotel on the second floor.

    Cole's claim is more confident and far less detailed. Its own site says "In 1908, Cole's, inventor of the French Dip sandwich and the oldest public house in Los Angeles was established by entrepreneur Harry Cole," and bills the restaurant as "Originators of the French Dip" — but gives no invention date and no account of how it happened. The Los Angeles Conservancy, describing the 1904 Huntington Building whose basement Cole's occupied from 1908, calls the restaurant the "self-proclaimed inventor of the French dip sandwich." That building held the offices and depot of Henry Huntington's Pacific Electric Railway, and Cole's opened as Cole's P.E. Buffet, feeding streetcar commuters.

    Cole's ran from 1908 until March 2026. Its owner, Cedd Moses, announced the closure in July 2025, citing rising labor, goods and rent costs, the lasting effects of the pandemic, the Hollywood strikes and crime; the last day was postponed several times before the restaurant finally shut.

    Timeline

    • 1908 — Both restaurants open in Los Angeles: Philippe's, founded by Philippe Mathieu, and Cole's, in the basement of the Huntington Building, where the Pacific Electric Railway kept its offices and depot.
    • 1918 — The year Philippe's own history gives for the dropped roll and the policeman who took the sandwich anyway.
    • 1927 — Harry, Dave and Frank Martin buy Philippe's from Mathieu for around $5,000.
    • 1951 — Philippe's is displaced by construction of the Hollywood-Santa Ana freeway and moves to its current Alameda Street location.
    • 2008 — Cole's reopens after a restoration costing a reported $1.6 million, led by Cedd Moses; it receives a Los Angeles Conservancy preservation award the following year.
    • 2025-2026 — Cole's announces in July 2025 that it will close, sets August 3 as its last day, postpones several times, and shuts in March 2026. New owners say they intend to bring it back.

    How to observe

    The useful thing to do with this day is eat both and decide for yourself — which until recently meant a short walk across downtown Los Angeles and now depends on whether Cole's actually reopens. Philippe's is still in the Alameda Street building it moved to in 1951. A lamb version with blue cheese is among its more popular orders, which is worth knowing, because the beef is what everyone defaults to.

    If you are nowhere near Los Angeles, the sandwich is easy to approximate and hard to improve on, because the whole point is the jus. Roast the beef, keep the pan liquid, skim the fat, pour it hot into a cup, and dip a plain French roll yourself.

    And if you would rather argue than eat, read the two restaurants' own websites side by side. Philippe's tells a story with a date in it. Cole's states a conclusion. That gap is the entire dispute, and it is the reason this page cannot tell you who is right.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who invented the French dip?

    It is not settled. Philippe the Original says Philippe Mathieu did, in 1918, when he dropped a roll into a hot roasting pan. Cole's says it did, without giving a year or an account of how. Both opened in 1908, and no source available to this page resolves the claim either way.

    Why is it called a French dip if it isn't French?

    Nobody knows. Philippe's own history says plainly that "the answer is lost to history." The usual explanations involve the French roll or the founder's name, and neither is documented.

    Who created National French Dip Day?

    No traceable source records who set it or when. The November 12 date appears only on daily-holiday calendars, which cite no origin and which this site does not treat as sources.

    Is Cole's still open?

    It closed in March 2026, after announcing in July 2025 that it would shut and then postponing the date several times. In October 2026, LAist reported that new owners planned to reopen it before Thanksgiving.

    What is actually in a French dip?

    Roast beef on a French roll with the juices from the roast, either dipped before serving or served alongside for dipping. At Philippe's, a lamb version with blue cheese is also a popular order.
