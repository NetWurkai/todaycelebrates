    National Hugging Day falls on January 21 and is one of the rare modern observances whose founder is both known and still running it. Kevin Zaborney created it in 1986 in Caro, Michigan, and placed it deliberately in late January, when the holidays are over and winter is not. Four decades on, its organizers spend as much effort on consent, and on alternatives for people who do not want to be touched, as on hugging itself.

    History

    National Hugging Day was founded in 1986 by Kevin Zaborney in Caro, Michigan, and the name is trademarked; National Hugging Day LLC runs it now. Zaborney's account is that the day spread after it was published in Chase's Calendar of Annual Events, the long-running reference of American observances — which is how one person's idea became a dated entry other people could find and mark. The organization states that the name was established in 1986 and formally registered with the U.S. Copyright Office in 1989, under the title "National Hugging Day Collection."

    The date was a choice rather than an accident. The organizers put it in late January specifically because of what that stretch of the calendar does to people: the holidays are finished, social contact drops off, and post-holiday flatness arrives in the coldest and quietest weeks of the year. The day is meant to sit in that gap.

    Two caveats belong here. The founding rests on a single source, the founder's own site, because no independent reputable account of the 1986 origin could be found. And the organization's reach claims — millions of participants across more than 100 countries — are its own figures rather than independently measured ones.

    What has changed most over forty years is the framing. The organizers now state the day plainly as consensual: ask first, and if the answer is no or you cannot predict it, do something else. They publish non-touch alternatives — heart-hand gestures, a note, a gentle high-five — aimed particularly at autistic and sensory-sensitive people, so that a day built on physical contact is not a day that quietly excludes them.

    The health claim usually attached to hugging has better evidence behind it than most holiday-page assertions, and less than the headlines suggest. In a study led by Sheldon Cohen at Carnegie Mellon and published in Psychological Science, 404 healthy adults were interviewed by telephone on 14 consecutive evenings about interpersonal conflicts and hugs received, then deliberately exposed to a common cold virus and monitored in quarantine. Perceived social support reduced the risk of infection associated with conflict, and hugs accounted for about one-third of that protective effect; among those who did get infected, more frequent hugs went with less severe symptoms. Cohen's own reading was careful — the effect may come from the physical contact itself, or from hugging simply being a reliable sign of a close and supportive relationship.

    How to observe

    Ask before hugging anyone whose reaction you cannot predict. The organizers say so themselves, and it is the whole difference between a day that works and a day people brace for.

    If touch is not for you, or not for the person you want to reach, use one of the substitutes the organizers publish: a heart-hand gesture, a card, a short message saying you were thinking of them. The point of the day is the signal, and the signal travels without contact.

    Pick someone outside your own household. Late January is when people living alone go longest without a real conversation, which is the gap the date was chosen to address. A phone call to someone you would otherwise only text does more work than a hug given to a person you already hug daily.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Hugging Day?

    January 21, every year. The date is fixed and does not move.

    Who started National Hugging Day?

    Kevin Zaborney, in 1986, in Caro, Michigan. He still runs it through National Hugging Day LLC. This page's source for the founding is Zaborney's own site, as no independent reputable account of the 1986 origin was available.

    Why is it in January?

    By design. The organizers chose late January because it falls after the holidays, when social contact drops and spirits are low, in the coldest and quietest part of the year.

    What if I do not want to be hugged?

    Then you should not be. The organizers ask people to seek consent first, and publish non-touch alternatives — heart-hand gestures, kindness notes, gentle high-fives — for autistic and sensory-sensitive people and anyone else who prefers them.

    Is there real evidence that hugs help?

    Some. The Carnegie Mellon study described above found that people reporting more frequent hugs were somewhat more protected against stress-related infection and had milder symptoms if infected. The researchers noted the effect might be the contact itself, or simply that hugging indicates a close, supportive relationship.
