    U.S. General Election Day falls on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, which pins it somewhere between November 2 and November 8. It is the day Americans elect members of Congress and, every fourth year, the presidential electors who formally choose the president. Three separate federal statutes, passed seventy years apart, are what put all of that on the same Tuesday.

    History

    For the republic's first half-century there was no national election day. The Act of March 1, 1792 directed that presidential electors be appointed "within thirty-four days preceding the first Wednesday in December," so voting could sprawl across more than a month, and states ran their congressional elections on dates of their own choosing. By the early 1800s more than half the states had drifted toward voting in early November anyway.

    The Act of January 23, 1845 fixed a single day. One sentence long, it provided that electors "shall be appointed in each State on the Tuesday next after the first Monday in the month of November of the year in which they are to be appointed." The motive stated on the House floor in 1844 was "to guard against frauds in the elections of President and Vice President": a thirty-four-day window let results from the early states travel to the late ones, and it prolonged the opportunity for mischief. Congress put House elections on the same Tuesday in 1872 — effective with the 1876 election, and tucked into an apportionment bill — and aligned the popular election of senators with it in 1914, after the Seventeenth Amendment took that choice away from state legislatures.

    Why that particular Tuesday is less settled than it is usually told. The familiar reasons — that early November sat between the harvest and the winter, that Tuesday spared voters from traveling to distant polls on the Sabbath, and that the first Tuesday after a Monday can never fall on November 1, when merchants closed out their books — come from later scholarship rather than from the 1845 record. The often-repeated claim that Congress was avoiding All Saints' Day has no reputable source behind it at all.

    Timeline

    • 1788–1789 — The first federal elections run from November 24, 1788 to June 22, 1789; electors cast their ballots in February 1789 and Congress counts them in April.
    • 1792 — Congress directs that presidential electors be appointed within the thirty-four days before the first Wednesday in December.
    • 1845 — The Act of January 23 fixes the Tuesday after the first Monday in November as the day for appointing electors in every state.
    • 1848 — November 7 is the first presidential election held on the same day nationwide.
    • 1872 — House elections are moved to the same November Tuesday, effective with the 1876 election.
    • 1914 — Congress aligns the popular election of senators with the biennial House elections.

    How to observe

    Check your registration well before the day rather than on it. Deadlines vary by state, a handful of states allow registration at the polls, and vote.gov links through to each state's own system. Then look up a sample ballot. Down-ballot judicial retention questions and ballot measures are where most voters arrive unprepared, and the text is published in advance.

    Consider working the election rather than only voting in it. States reported 772,433 poll workers for the 2024 general election, and more than half of them were 61 or older; the Election Assistance Commission's poll-worker page lists requirements, hours, pay, training and local sign-up. If you do only one thing, confirm your polling place and its hours — both change between elections more often than people expect.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Election Day?

    It is the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, so it moves between November 2 and November 8. The next federal general election is November 3, 2026.

    Is Election Day a federal holiday?

    No. The eleven legal public holidays are listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103, and Election Day is not among them; federal offices keep normal hours. Federal employees may be granted excused absence to vote, but only in limited circumstances. A few states, including Virginia and Delaware, do make it a state legal holiday, and many more give employees statutory time off to vote — which is a different thing from a holiday.

    Why is Election Day on a Tuesday in November?

    Congress set the uniform date in 1845 to curb the fraud that staggered state voting made easy. The familiar harvest-and-Sabbath explanations are reconstructions by later historians rather than reasons Congress recorded at the time.

    How many Americans actually vote?

    For the 2024 general election, states reported 158,211,780 ballots cast and counted — 64.7 percent of the citizen voting-age population, by the Election Assistance Commission's tally of certified results. The Census Bureau's survey-based estimate for the same election is higher, at 65.3 percent, and the Bureau notes that surveys tend to report higher voting rates than official results do.

    Can Election Day be moved?

    Not administratively. The date is set by statute, so changing it would take a new act of Congress; no president or federal official has the power to move it.
