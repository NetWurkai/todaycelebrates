    Cook Something Bold Day falls on November 8 and is an instruction rather than a commemoration: make the thing that fills the whole house with smell. Its own origin cannot be sourced, so this page is deliberately short, and spends its words on the part of the subject that is documented — how cooks and chemists actually measure "bold."

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, proclamation, or first observance could be sourced. The day circulates under two names, Cook Something Bold Day and Cook Something Bold and Pungent Day, and both appear only on daily-holiday aggregators and blogs that cite one another, which is not a source. Rather than attach a plausible-sounding founder to it, this page says so and moves on.

    "Bold" in cooking usually means pungent, and pungency is one of the few qualities of flavor with a real measurement history. In 1912 the pharmacist Wilbur Scoville published a short paper, "Note on Capsicums," in the Journal of the American Pharmaceutical Association, proposing a way to rate the heat of peppers. As the Alimentarium, the food museum in Vevey, Switzerland, describes the method, Scoville prepared a pepper in solution and had five people taste it, increasing the dilution until the sensation of heat disappeared; the score is the dilution at which the heat vanishes completely. The scale still carries his name, and heat is still quoted in Scoville heat units.

    It is a subjective test by design — five palates and one verdict — which is why it has been supplemented rather than simply trusted. Guinness World Records, which adjudicates the hottest chili pepper, states that the record is measured in both Scoville heat units and American Spice Trade Association pungency units, and that chili seeds are not accepted for the category. The compound doing the work is capsaicin, which the Alimentarium links directly to a pepper's pungency; the National Institutes of Health's PubChem database gives its molecular formula as C18H27NO3.

    How to observe

    Cook something that announces itself from the doorway. Roast a head of garlic whole. Toast whole spices in a dry pan before grinding them, which is the single cheapest upgrade in home cooking and takes ninety seconds. Braise something with anchovy in it and do not tell anyone until they ask what makes it taste like that.

    Build heat in layers rather than at the end. A dried chili bloomed in oil at the start, a fresh one added late, and a splash of something acidic to finish will read as deeper than the same total quantity dumped in at once.

    Then write down what you did. Pungency is measured by dilution for a reason: the threshold at which heat disappears is personal, and the only way to find yours is to keep notes on what you actually enjoyed rather than what you survived. Opening a window is optional and your neighbors may disagree.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who created Cook Something Bold Day?

    Not documented. No founding organization, proclamation, or first observance could be sourced, and the day appears only on daily-holiday aggregators and blogs.

    What counts as "bold"?

    There is no official definition, because there is no founding body to set one. The day's longer circulating name, Cook Something Bold and Pungent Day, points at pungency — garlic, chili, mustard, fermented and aromatic things — rather than at heat alone.

    What is a Scoville heat unit?

    It is a dilution figure. In the original 1912 test a pepper was prepared in solution and tasted by five people, with the dilution increased until the heat could no longer be felt; the score is the dilution required for that.

    Is the Scoville scale still used?

    Yes. Guinness World Records measures its hottest chili pepper record in Scoville heat units alongside American Spice Trade Association pungency units.

    Does bold have to mean spicy?

    No. Pungency covers garlic, onion, horseradish, mustard, aged cheese, and fermented foods as much as chilies, none of which register on a heat scale built for capsicums.
