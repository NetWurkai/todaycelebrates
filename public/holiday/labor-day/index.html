    Celebration

    Labor Day falls on the first Monday in September and honors the American labor movement and the people whose work built the country. It began as a union parade in New York City on a Tuesday in September 1882, spread one state legislature at a time through the 1880s, and became a federal holiday in June 1894 — in the middle of a national railroad strike that federal troops had been sent to break. The timing is part of the story, not a coincidence.

    History

    Who first proposed Labor Day is genuinely unsettled, and the U.S. Department of Labor says so on its own history page. One candidate is Peter J. McGuire, general secretary of the Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners and a co-founder of the American Federation of Labor, reported to have called in 1882 for a general holiday for the laboring classes. The other is Matthew Maguire, a machinist who later served as secretary of Local 344 in Paterson, New Jersey, and whom many credit with proposing the holiday that same year while secretary of the Central Labor Union in New York. A Paterson newspaper called Maguire "the undisputed author of Labor Day as a holiday." The near-identical surnames have not helped, and the Labor Department declines to pick.

    What is documented is the event. The Central Labor Union held the first Labor Day in New York City on Tuesday, September 5, 1882, and a second a year later on the same date. The plan was a street parade showing the strength and esprit de corps of a community's trade and labor organizations, followed by a festival for workers and their families. Speeches came later.

    The states moved first. Oregon passed the first Labor Day law on February 21, 1887; Colorado, Massachusetts, New Jersey and New York followed within the year, and Connecticut, Nebraska and Pennsylvania by the decade's end. By 1894 another 23 states had adopted it.

    Congress acted mid-strike. The Pullman strike ran from May 11 to July 20, 1894; President Grover Cleveland declared it a federal crime and sent troops, and two men were killed. On June 28, 1894, Congress made the first Monday in September a legal holiday and Cleveland signed it. The Library of Congress connects that speed to the need to calm tensions after the deaths.

    Timeline

    • September 5, 1882 — The Central Labor Union holds the first Labor Day in New York City, on a Tuesday: a street parade followed by a festival for workers and their families.
    • September 5, 1883 — The Central Labor Union holds its second Labor Day on the same date.
    • February 21, 1887 — Oregon passes the first state Labor Day law. Colorado, Massachusetts, New Jersey and New York follow the same year.
    • May 11, 1894 — The Pullman strike begins, running until July 20. Cleveland declares it a federal crime and sends troops; two men are killed.
    • June 28, 1894 — Congress makes the first Monday in September a legal holiday and President Grover Cleveland signs it. By then 23 more states had adopted the holiday.
    • June 28, 1968 — The Uniform Monday Holiday Act (Pub. L. 90-363) reworks the federal calendar, adding Columbus Day and moving Washington's Birthday, Memorial Day and Veterans Day. Labor Day is not among the holidays it changes.

    How to observe

    Read the Department of Labor's own history of the holiday. It is short, and it refuses to settle who founded Labor Day — which makes it a better document than most of what is written about the day.

    Visit Pullman National Historical Park if you are near Chicago. The planned industrial town at the center of the 1894 strike still stands as a neighborhood, and the Park Service runs a visitor center at 610 E. 111th Street. The holiday and the strike are the same year; standing in one explains the other.

    Look up what your own town did. The Library of Congress's Chronicling America holds digitized American newspapers published through 1963 from nearly every state and territory. Searching your city against the 1880s and 1890s turns up the parades, picnics and arguments as they were reported — the only way to see a holiday that arrived one state at a time.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Labor Day a federal holiday?

    Yes. It is one of the eleven legal public holidays listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103, observed on the first Monday in September. Federal offices, banks and post offices close.

    Who invented Labor Day?

    Nobody knows for certain, and the Department of Labor says so. The two candidates are Peter J. McGuire, general secretary of the Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners, and Matthew Maguire, a machinist from Paterson, New Jersey. Both are tied to proposals in 1882, and the nearly identical surnames have kept the question open ever since.

    When was the first Labor Day?

    Tuesday, September 5, 1882, in New York City, organized by the Central Labor Union. It was a parade and a festival, not a day off.

    Why is Labor Day in September rather than May 1?

    There is no primary source for that decision in the record this page relies on, so it will not guess at one. What is documented is that the September date came from the Central Labor Union's 1882 celebration and was already law in more than twenty states before Congress adopted it in 1894.

    When did Labor Day become a federal holiday?

    June 28, 1894. Congress made the first Monday in September a legal holiday and President Cleveland signed it, during the Pullman strike that his own administration had sent troops to break.
