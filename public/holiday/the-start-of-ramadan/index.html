    The Start of Ramadan

    Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar and the month in which observant Muslims fast from dawn to sunset, one of the five Pillars of Islam. Its first day is not a fixed date but a determination, made fresh each year from a crescent moon that different authorities read in different ways — which is why mosques in the same American city sometimes begin on different days. Because the lunar year runs about eleven days shorter than the solar one, it moves backward through the seasons, returning to where it started roughly every thirty-three years.

    History

    The fast is prescribed in the Quran. Surah al-Baqarah 2:183 establishes fasting for believers as it was for communities before them; 2:184 provides that those who are ill or traveling make the days up later, while those who cannot fast may feed a needy person for each day missed; 2:185 names Ramadan as the month in which the Quran was revealed and says that God intends ease, not hardship. Tradition dates that first revelation to Laylat al-Qadr, around 610 CE, the night the last ten nights of the month commemorate. The daily boundary comes from 2:187: eat and drink until the first light of dawn can be told from the darkness, then complete the fast until night. Dawn, not sunrise — usually an hour or more earlier, a distinction some reference works get wrong.

    The movement through the seasons is a function of the calendar. The Hijri year is twelve lunar months of 29 or 30 days, totaling 354 or 355, counted from the Hijrah of 622 CE. Because that year is roughly eleven days shorter than the solar one, the named months retrogress through the seasonal year about every 32.5 years.

    The disagreement over when Ramadan begins is a question of religious law, not of science against superstition, and it is the thing most often described badly. Tradition starts the month on a naked-eye sighting of the crescent, the most conservative position requiring two credible witnesses. In 2006 the Fiqh Council of North America announced it would set dates by astronomical calculation instead, and the Islamic Society of North America adopted calculation the same year. Its criterion, taken from the European Council for Fatwa and Research, is that if anywhere on earth at sunset the moon's elongation is at least 8 degrees and its altitude at least 5 degrees, the new month begins the next day. Other mosques and the Central Hilal Committee of North America still prefer sighting, and the Fiqh Council accepts other opinions and encourages Muslims to follow their local masjid. As Imam Dawud Walid has put it, unity does not equal uniformity.

    Timeline

    • 622 CE — The Hijrah, Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina, becomes the epoch of the Hijri calendar; year 1 begins on 1 Muharram, 16 July 622 in the Julian calendar. The caliph Umar I institutes the era in 639.
    • 2006 — The Fiqh Council of North America announces it will set Islamic dates by astronomical calculation rather than moon sighting, and the Islamic Society of North America adopts calculation the same year.
    • February 18, 2026 — 1 Ramadan 1447: the Fiqh Council's calculated date and Saudi Arabia's moon-sighting committee land on the same day.
    • January 5 and December 26, 2030 — Ramadan begins twice in one Gregorian year, the clearest demonstration of the drift.

    How to observe

    Two meals bracket the fasting day: suhur before dawn and iftar after sunset. Iftar follows the sunset prayer, is usually eaten with family or at a mosque, and traditionally begins with dates, after the Prophet's custom. Nights bring taraweeh, lengthy congregational prayers. Charity is a hallmark of the month — iftar for the needy, boxes of pantry staples, free communal meals — and zakat al-fitr is an obligatory payment at its end. Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, falls somewhere in the last ten nights, marked on the 27th by Sunnis and the 23rd by Shia. Eid al-Fitr ends the month.

    If you work alongside someone who is fasting, the useful thing to know is the law: Title VII requires employers to reasonably accommodate a sincerely held religious practice absent undue hardship, and the EEOC states plainly that coworker objections rooted in hostility to religion, or customer prejudice, do not count as undue hardship.

    Frequently asked questions

    When does Ramadan begin?

    Not something settled far ahead. The Fiqh Council of North America publishes a calculated date each year, while national committees and many individual mosques wait for a sighting. Any Ramadan date printed years out, including the one in the table on this page, is a projection rather than an announcement.

    Why does the date move?

    Twelve lunar months come to 354 or 355 days, about eleven short of the solar year, so Ramadan arrives ten to twelve days earlier each Gregorian year and works through every season in roughly 33 years. It falls twice in 2030.

    Is it really no food or water all day?

    Both, from dawn to sunset, along with marital relations during fasting hours. But Muslims eat every day of Ramadan, before dawn and after sunset; the common line that they go a month without eating is simply wrong.

    Who is exempt from fasting?

    Children, the ill, travelers, pregnant and nursing women, the elderly, and people with disabilities or mental health conditions. These are provisions of the religion rather than loopholes: the Quran allows the days to be made up later, or a poor person fed for each one missed.
