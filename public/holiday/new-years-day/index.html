    New Year's Day

    New Year's Day falls on January 1, which is an administrative decision rather than a fact of nature. The date has been moved, abandoned, and restored more than once in Western Europe — Britain and its American colonies did not adopt it until 1752 — and most of what Americans actually do on the day, from the parade in Pasadena to the ball in Times Square, was invented between 1788 and 1907.

    History

    January 1 has no astronomical claim on the new year. Tradition credits Numa Pompilius, around 715 to 673 BCE, with reordering the Roman calendar so that January replaced March as the first month, though that attribution is traditional rather than documented; there is evidence that January 1 was not made the official start of the Roman year until 153 BCE. Julius Caesar's reform of 46 BCE produced the Julian calendar, which kept January 1 as the year's opening date.

    Then it moved. After the fifth century much of Christian Europe shifted the new year off January 1, and March 25 and December 25 both became common starting dates. Pope Gregory XIII's calendar of 1582 restored January 1; Italy, France, and Spain adopted it immediately, while Protestant and Orthodox countries took far longer. Britain and its American colonies did not switch until 1752, having kept New Year's Day on March 25 until then — which makes the January 1 holiday younger, in English-speaking America, than most of the colonies observing it.

    Legal standing is younger still. Congress first established public holidays in 1870, for the District of Columbia: New Year's Day, Independence Day, Christmas, and Thanksgiving.

    The two American set pieces are both promotional in origin and both well documented. Times Square got its crowd in 1904, when Adolph Ochs staged an all-day street festival with fireworks to mark the opening of The New York Times' new headquarters — he had already persuaded the city to rename Longacre Square after the paper — and more than 200,000 people came. The city banned the fireworks two years later, and in 1907 a lighted ball descended the flagpole atop One Times Square instead: iron and wood, five feet across, 700 pounds, one hundred 25-watt bulbs, built by a young immigrant metalworker named Jacob Starr. It has gone down every year since except 1942 and 1943, when New York's wartime dimout darkened the square. The ball unveiled in November 2025 is 12.5 feet across and weighs 12,350 pounds.

    Pasadena's parade is older and was frank about its purpose. Members of the Valley Hunt Club, led by Charles Frederick Holder, staged the first Tournament of Roses on New Year's Day in 1890. Holder's pitch to the club was advertising copy: "In New York, people are buried in snow. Let's hold a festival to tell the world about our paradise."

    Timeline

    • 153 BCE — Evidence indicates January 1 becomes the official start of the Roman year.
    • 46 BCE — Julius Caesar's reform produces the Julian calendar, which keeps January 1 as the year's opening date.
    • 1582 — Pope Gregory XIII's calendar restores January 1; Italy, France, and Spain adopt it immediately.
    • 1752 — Britain and its American colonies adopt the Gregorian calendar, having observed New Year's Day on March 25 until then.
    • 1870 — Congress establishes the first federal public holidays for the District of Columbia, New Year's Day among them.
    • 1907 — A lighted ball descends One Times Square for the first time, after the city banned the fireworks of the 1904 celebration.

    How to observe

    Find out when your New Year's Day off actually falls. Federal employees follow 5 U.S.C. 6103, and when January 1 lands on a Saturday the holiday is observed the preceding Friday. The federal New Year's Day for 2028 is therefore Friday, December 31, 2027.

    Sing the song wrong knowingly. Robert Burns did not so much write "Auld Lang Syne" as compile it, in 1788, from older material reaching back as far as the sixteenth century. The tune was his publisher's choice and derives from a traditional Scottish folk-dance tune, possibly Sir Alexander Don's strathspey, though the Library of Congress notes the exact source is difficult to establish.

    Watch the Rose Parade knowing what it was for. The Tournament of Roses began in 1890 as a winter-climate advertisement for Southern California, and the man who proposed it said so out loud at the meeting that launched it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is New Year's Day a federal holiday?

    Yes, and one of the original four. Congress first established public holidays in 1870 — New Year's Day, Independence Day, Christmas, and Thanksgiving — for the District of Columbia. It is now listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103.

    What happens when January 1 falls on a weekend?

    Federal employees observe the holiday on the nearest weekday: the preceding Friday when January 1 is a Saturday, the following Monday when it is a Sunday. January 1, 2028 is a Saturday, so the federal observance is Friday, December 31, 2027.

    Why does the year start on January 1?

    Because it was legislated that way, repeatedly. Rome appears to have fixed January 1 as the official new year by 153 BCE, and the Julian calendar of 46 BCE kept it. Medieval Christian Europe largely moved the date to March 25 or December 25. The Gregorian calendar of 1582 restored January 1, and Britain and its colonies followed only in 1752.

    Has the Times Square ball ever not dropped?

    Twice. It was not lowered in 1942 or 1943, when New York's wartime dimout darkened the square. Crowds gathered anyway and marked midnight with a minute of silence and chimes rung from sound trucks at the base of the tower.

    Where do New Year's resolutions come from?

    The custom is usually traced to Babylonian or Roman vow-making, but that lineage is asserted far more often than it is documented, and this page has no primary source for it. Resolving at the turn of the year is an old habit; its specific ancestry is not established.
