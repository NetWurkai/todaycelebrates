    Family Health History Day is one of the rare observances on this calendar with a documented founder, a documented date and a stated purpose. In November 2004 the U.S. Surgeon General, Richard H. Carmona, declared Thanksgiving Day the first annual National Family History Day, on the reasoning that the one day a year when an extended family is in the same room is the day to ask relatives what they have been diagnosed with and what their parents died of. Because it is pinned to Thanksgiving rather than to a fixed date, it moves: in 2026 it falls on November 26.

    History

    On November 8, 2004, Health and Human Services Secretary Tommy G. Thompson launched a Family History Initiative, and, in the department's own announcement, "U.S. Surgeon General Richard H. Carmona declared Thanksgiving Day, when American families traditionally gather to celebrate and give thanks, to be the first annual National Family History Day." The initiative shipped with a tool: My Family Health Portrait, a free computer program for recording a household's history across six common diseases, among them heart disease, cancer and diabetes. The case for the campaign came from a CDC Healthstyles survey taken that August, which found that 96 percent of Americans believed knowing their family history was important to their health, while only one-third had ever tried to gather and organize it.

    The idea had a running start inside the government. CDC's National Office of Public Health Genomics had launched a Family History Public Health Initiative in 2002. Carmona unveiled a web-based version of the tool on November 15, 2005, for what the announcement called the second annual National Family History Day, reporting more than 360,000 downloads of the original program in its first year and more than 85,000 printed copies distributed. The partner agencies were the National Human Genome Research Institute, CDC, the Health Resources and Services Administration and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality.

    CDC kept the day alive after the initiative's own publicity faded. Its Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report carried notices in 2007 and 2016; the 2016 announcement is where the arithmetic gets concrete, noting that having at least one first-degree relative with a disease "can increase a person's risk twofold or more," and that an estimated 20 percent of women with family histories of breast and ovarian cancer may carry BRCA mutations. On September 6, 2018, My Family Health Portrait moved from the genome institute, which had hosted it for more than a decade, to CDC's Public Health Genomics Knowledge Base.

    Two details have never settled. The name is given three ways by the agencies themselves: National Family History Day in the 2004 announcement and in CDC's weekly report, National Family Health History Day by the genome institute, and Family Health History Day in CDC's 2018 post. And the launch date is told two ways: CDC's 2018 retrospective says the Surgeon General launched the campaign "On Thanksgiving Day 2004," while the contemporaneous HHS announcement is dated November 8, 2004, more than two weeks before that Thanksgiving. This page follows the announcement, as the nearer record.

    How to observe

    Collect the specific things CDC asks for, which is narrower and more answerable than "family history": major medical conditions, causes of death, the age at which each disease was diagnosed, age at death, and ethnic background. Ask about parents, siblings and half-siblings, children, grandparents, aunts and uncles, nieces and nephews.

    Use the gathering itself, which is the entire point of the Thanksgiving pinning, and fill the gaps afterward from death certificates and family medical records.

    Write it down somewhere a doctor can read it. The Surgeon General's My Family Health Portrait tool still exists and is linked from CDC's family health history pages. A paper list works as well.

    Take it to an appointment even if it is patchy. CDC's guidance is explicit that an incomplete history is still worth bringing, because it helps a clinician decide which screening tests you need and when they should start.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Family Health History Day?

    It is observed on Thanksgiving Day, so the date moves each year. In 2026 that is Thursday, November 26.

    Who created it?

    U.S. Surgeon General Richard H. Carmona, who declared Thanksgiving the first annual National Family History Day as part of a Family History Initiative that HHS launched on November 8, 2004.

    What exactly should I write down?

    CDC asks for major medical conditions, causes of death, the age at diagnosis for each disease, age at death, and ethnic background, for parents, siblings and half-siblings, children, grandparents, aunts and uncles, nieces and nephews.

    Is a family health history the same thing as genetic testing?

    No. CDC describes a family health history as a record of the diseases and health conditions in your family, covering not only shared genes but shared behaviors and environment, any of which can affect your health.

    Why does it matter if a relative had a disease?

    CDC's weekly report puts it plainly: having at least one first-degree relative with a disease can increase a person's risk twofold or more. The practical use is timing — family history helps a clinician decide which screening tests you need and when they should start.
