    Memorial Day

    Memorial Day falls on the last Monday in May and honors Americans who died in military service — which is the distinction that matters, because Veterans Day honors everyone who served. It began as Decoration Day, a Civil War observance of strewing flowers on graves, and carried both names for most of a century. Where it started first is genuinely disputed, and the federal government's own answer is not the only credible one.

    History

    The holiday's official origin is a general order. On May 5, 1868, John A. Logan, commander-in-chief of the Grand Army of the Republic — the Union veterans' organization — issued General Orders No. 11, setting aside May 30 for "strewing with flowers or otherwise decorating the graves of comrades" and asking that the country guard those graves "with sacred vigilance." Late May was chosen for the flowers: the order wanted "the choicest flowers of springtime." Logan's adjutant general, Norton P. Chipman, drafted it; Logan added several paragraphs of his own.

    The first national observance followed on May 30, 1868, at Arlington National Cemetery, on grounds that then held roughly 11,250 Union dead, Black and white, and about 350 Confederate dead, more than half of them unknown. General and Mrs. Ulysses S. Grant were among those presiding, James A. Garfield — then both a general and a congressman — spoke, and the Department of Veterans Affairs puts the crowd at about 5,000.

    The practice was older than the order, and the claims are crowded. Southern women were decorating soldiers' graves before the war ended, with precedents recorded in Mississippi, Virginia and South Carolina by 1865. On April 25, 1866, a women's memorial association in Columbus, Mississippi, decorated Confederate graves and the neglected Union graves beside them. Waterloo, New York, holds the federal title — Congress and President Lyndon Johnson named it the birthplace of Memorial Day in 1966, for observances beginning May 5, 1866 — while Boalsburg, Carbondale, Macon, Columbus and Richmond all have claims of their own. Earlier still, on May 1, 1865, freed Black residents of Charleston, South Carolina, together with soldiers of the 35th and 104th United States Colored Troops and the 54th Massachusetts Infantry, gathered at the city's old racetrack to honor and decorate the graves of 257 Union prisoners of war whom the retreating Confederate army had buried there in haste. The National Park Service calls it one of the earliest recorded observances and says the question of which came first is still debated.

    Decoration Day gave way to Memorial Day around the turn of the twentieth century, without a decisive moment. The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1968 took effect in 1971, moving the holiday to the last Monday in May and extending it to Americans who died in all the nation's wars; a few states still mark May 30. In December 2000 the National Moment of Remembrance Act created the commission behind a minute of silence at 3 p.m. local time.

    Timeline

    • May 1, 1865 — Freed Black residents of Charleston, South Carolina, with Black and white Union troops, honor and decorate the graves of 257 Union prisoners of war at the city's old racetrack — among the earliest recorded observances.
    • May 5, 1866 — Waterloo, New York, begins the annual observance that Congress and President Johnson would cite in 1966 in naming it the birthplace of Memorial Day.
    • May 5, 1868 — John A. Logan, commander-in-chief of the Grand Army of the Republic, issues General Orders No. 11, setting May 30 aside for decorating the graves of the war dead.
    • May 30, 1868 — The first national Decoration Day is held at Arlington National Cemetery, with the Grants among those presiding and James A. Garfield speaking.
    • 1971 — The Uniform Monday Holiday Act takes effect, moving the holiday to the last Monday in May and extending it to the dead of all American wars.
    • December 2000 — The National Moment of Remembrance Act (Public Law 106-579) establishes the commission behind the 3 p.m. minute of silence.

    How to observe

    Take the 3 p.m. minute. It is the one part of this holiday with a fixed national time, and, unlike a parade, it asks nothing but that you stop.

    Visit a national cemetery instead of a sale. The Department of Veterans Affairs' National Cemetery Administration keeps cemeteries in most states and publishes their locations; many hold a ceremony and a flag placement over the holiday weekend.

    Read Logan's order. It is short, it is the document the holiday rests on, and its instruction — that the graves be guarded "with sacred vigilance" and kept tended for the generations that follow — is a plainer statement of the point than most of what gets said on the day.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Memorial Day a federal holiday?

    Yes. It is one of the eleven legal public holidays listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103 and falls on the last Monday in May, so its date moves between May 25 and May 31.

    What is the difference between Memorial Day and Veterans Day?

    Memorial Day honors military personnel who died in service, particularly those killed in battle or from wounds sustained in battle. Veterans Day, on November 11, honors everyone who served honorably, and is largely a day for thanking living veterans.

    Where did Memorial Day start?

    There is no settled answer. The federal government named Waterloo, New York the birthplace in 1966; the National Park Service records a May 1, 1865 observance in Charleston, South Carolina as one of the earliest; and Boalsburg, Carbondale, Columbus, Macon and Richmond all have claims. What is documented is the start of the national observance: Logan's order of May 5, 1868 and the ceremony at Arlington that May 30.

    When did Memorial Day stop being May 30?

    The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1968 took effect in 1971, moving the observance to the last Monday in May and extending it to Americans who died in all the nation's wars. A few states still mark May 30.

    What is the National Moment of Remembrance?

    A minute of silence at 3 p.m. local time on Memorial Day. The National Moment of Remembrance Act of December 2000 created the White House commission that endorsed it.
