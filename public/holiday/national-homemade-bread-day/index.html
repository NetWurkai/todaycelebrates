    National Homemade Bread Day falls on November 17, and no organization, agency, or publication can be found claiming to have started it. What is documented is more interesting than a founding story: bread is among the most closely defined staples in American food law, specified down to the weight at which a loaf stops being a loaf. This page covers the part the record supports.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding body claims it, no proclamation designates it, and the earliest mentions that surface are on daily-holiday aggregators, which this site does not cite. The date has no traceable author.

    What does have a paper trail is bread itself — the long argument over what the word is allowed to mean. The Food and Drug Administration's own history of its standards-of-identity work records that the earliest bread hearings began in 1941, and that "World War Two then intervened and these standards were put on hold."

    The war changed the subject rather than pausing it. By FDA's account, "during the war, bread was subject to a war food order mandating enrichment" — adding B vitamins and iron to replace what milling strips out. The agency's history cites Federal Security Administrator v. Quaker Oats Co., 318 U.S. 218 (1943), in which the Supreme Court upheld the government's approach: a manufacturer followed the mandated formula or stopped enriching the food.

    Afterward FDA chose not to require enrichment at all. It "elected not to mandate enrichment, but rather to write separate standards for enriched and for non-enriched products" — which is why American bread still comes in two legal kinds — and credits that structure with helping "eliminate a number of nutritional deficiency diseases in the post-war era, particularly in southern states." By 1957 standards were in place for flour and for bakery products.

    They remain in force as 21 CFR Part 136. Under section 136.3 the word "bread" applies where "the unit weighs one-half pound or more after cooling"; below that it is a roll or a bun. Under section 136.110 bread is produced by baking "mixed yeast-leavened dough," and each finished food "contains not less than 62 percent total solids." Under section 136.115 a loaf may be called enriched bread when each pound holds 1.8 milligrams of thiamin, 1.1 milligrams of riboflavin, 15 milligrams of niacin, 0.43 milligrams of folic acid, and 12.5 milligrams of iron.

    One patent marks the moment homemade stopped being the default: Otto F. Rohwedder of Davenport, Iowa filed on November 26, 1928 for a "Machine for slicing an entire loaf of bread at a single operation," issued as U.S. Patent 1,867,377 on July 12, 1932. The familiar story that packaged sliced bread first went on sale in Chillicothe, Missouri in 1928 is told nearly everywhere but not in any source this site accepts.

    Timeline

    • November 26, 1928 — Otto F. Rohwedder of Davenport, Iowa files for a "Machine for slicing an entire loaf of bread at a single operation."
    • July 12, 1932 — That application issues as U.S. Patent 1,867,377.
    • 1938 — The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act gives FDA authority to set standards of identity for staple foods.
    • 1941 — FDA's first bread hearings begin; the war puts the standards on hold, and a war food order mandates enrichment in the meantime.
    • 1943 — In Federal Security Administrator v. Quaker Oats Co., 318 U.S. 218, the Supreme Court upholds the government's approach to mandated enrichment formulas.
    • 1957 — Standards for flour and bakery products are in place, after FDA decides to write separate enriched and non-enriched standards rather than require enrichment of all bread.

    How to observe

    Weigh the loaf once it has cooled. At half a pound or more you have made bread as federal regulation defines it; under that, rolls. It is probably the only cooking rule that comes with a citation.

    Read the flour bag. If it says "enriched," the figures in section 136.115 are why, and they are stated per pound of finished product rather than per bag.

    For period recipes, search the Library of Congress's Chronicling America newspaper archive: a loaf from your own town in the 1890s beats a modern reconstruction of one.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is National Homemade Bread Day an official holiday?

    No. It has no federal or state recognition and no proclamation names it.

    Who started National Homemade Bread Day?

    Nobody identifiable from an acceptable source. No organization claims the day and no government document designates it. Any page naming a founder or a founding year for it is repeating an aggregator.

    What legally counts as bread in the United States?

    Under 21 CFR 136.110, bread is produced by baking mixed yeast-leavened dough from the ingredients the regulation lists, and the finished food must contain not less than 62 percent total solids. Under 21 CFR 136.3 the unit must also weigh at least half a pound after cooling, or it is a roll or a bun.

    Why is so much American bread enriched?

    It began as a wartime requirement: FDA's history records a war food order mandating enrichment. Afterward the agency wrote separate standards for enriched and non-enriched products instead, and credits them with helping eliminate nutritional deficiency diseases in the post-war southern states.

    What has to be in enriched bread?

    Per 21 CFR 136.115, each pound must contain 1.8 milligrams of thiamin, 1.1 milligrams of riboflavin, 15 milligrams of niacin, 0.43 milligrams of folic acid, and 12.5 milligrams of iron.
