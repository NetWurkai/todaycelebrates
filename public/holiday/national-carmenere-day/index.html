    November 24 marks Carménère Day, which commemorates a case of mistaken identity. For more than a century Chilean growers tended a Bordeaux grape they believed was Merlot; in 1994 a French ampelographer looked at the vines and said they were Carménère, a variety phylloxera and poor fruit set had all but erased in France. The trade body Wines of Chile launched the day in 2014, on the twentieth anniversary of that correction.

    History

    Carménère is an old French cultivar, thought to descend from a cross of Cabernet Franc and Gros Cabernet, and it was widely planted in the Bordeaux districts of Graves and Médoc before phylloxera devastated French vineyards in the nineteenth century. It did not come back there. As a 2026 study in OENO One puts it, its susceptibility to coulure, its late ripening and its low yields against Merlot "made it a poor candidate for replanting," and it had nearly vanished from European vineyards by the early twentieth century.

    Chile is where it survived, by accident of timing and geography. Cuttings arrived in the 1850s along with other Bordeaux varieties, just before phylloxera reached Europe, and Chile's isolation kept the pest out, so ungrafted pre-epidemic plant material persisted. What did not persist was the name. The vines were grown, sold and exported as Merlot — "Merlot Chileno" — until 1994, when the French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot noted in the field that they were not Merlot at all.

    Confirmation took longer and came from a laboratory. In 2001, Patricio Hinrichsen and colleagues, Boursiquot among them, published a short paper in the American Journal of Enology and Viticulture reporting DNA typing of 93 vines from five Chilean vineyards planted as Merlot, plus four vines from a cultivar collection that "matched Carmenère, although they were originally planted as Merlot." Two microsatellite markers, VVMD28 and VVMD31, proved particularly useful for telling Carménère from Merlot. Chile officially recognized Carménère as a distinct cultivar in 1998, and today more than 10,000 hectares are planted nationwide — a figure both recent genome studies give as a round "more than," without an exact number, citing Chile's agricultural service and the OIV.

    The holiday is younger than the DNA work. Wines of Chile announced it in November 2014, pegged to the twentieth anniversary of the rediscovery; Marybeth Bentwood, then director of Wines of Chile in the US, said the body was "thrilled to launch #CarmenereDay on Monday, November 24th." Two caveats follow. No source consulted explains why November 24 rather than another date in 1994 — the papers give the year, never the day. And though this page is titled National Carmenere Day, every source found calls it Carménère Day or World Carménère Day: it is a Chilean and international designation, not an American one. Wines of Chile's own site carries no page about it now; a site search this week returned nothing.

    Timeline

    • 1850s — Carménère cuttings reach Chile with other Bordeaux varieties, just before phylloxera hits Europe.
    • Late 1800s — Phylloxera devastates French vineyards; Carménère is largely eliminated there and nearly gone from Europe by the early 1900s.
    • 1994 — The French ampelographer Jean-Michel Boursiquot observes in the field that Chile's "Merlot" is Carménère.
    • 1998 — Chile officially recognizes Carménère as a distinct cultivar.
    • 2001 — Hinrichsen and colleagues publish DNA typing in the American Journal of Enology and Viticulture, confirming the identification and naming the markers that separate the two varieties.
    • November 24, 2014 — Wines of Chile launches Carménère Day on the twentieth anniversary of the rediscovery.

    How to observe

    Buy a bottle of Chilean Carménère and read the back label before the front. Because the variety ripens late and sets fruit unreliably, vintage and site matter more than they do for a sturdier grape, so the valley and the year are the useful information.

    If you are in Chile, ask a vineyard whether its vines are own-rooted. Carménère exists at all because Chilean vineyards escaped phylloxera and never had to be grafted, and the oldest plantings there trace to material imported from France around 1870.

    For the primary record, the 2001 identification paper runs four pages and its abstract is public, and the grape's genome has been published twice: a diploid assembly in G3 in 2019 and a study of Chilean clonal diversity in OENO One in 2026. Either is a reminder that "rediscovered in 1994" was a field observation that took seven more years to prove.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Carménère a Chilean grape or a French one?

    French in origin, Chilean in survival. It is an old Bordeaux cultivar largely wiped out in France by phylloxera, and it persisted in Chile, where cuttings arrived in the 1850s and the pest never took hold.

    Why was Carménère mistaken for Merlot?

    The Chilean plantings were grown and sold as Merlot. Boursiquot's 1994 correction was an observation of the vines themselves; DNA typing published in 2001 confirmed it and named two microsatellite markers that separate the varieties.

    Who created Carménère Day?

    Wines of Chile, the country's wine trade body, which announced it in November 2014 for November 24 and tied it to the twentieth anniversary of the rediscovery.

    Is it National Carménère Day or World Carménère Day?

    Sources call it Carménère Day or World Carménère Day. It comes from the Chilean industry, not an American body, so "National" is a label this site inherited rather than one the sponsor uses.
