    National Peanut Butter Fudge Day is marked on November 20, and no organization appears to claim it. What can be documented is the pair of things its name joins: a candy whose earliest recorded appearance is at an American women's college in the 1880s, and a ground-peanut paste patented in 1884 as an ingredient for candy. The observance is undocumented; the confection is not.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, proclamation, government page or news report establishes who designated November 20 or when; the pages carrying the date are daily-holiday aggregators, which are not sources. The two halves of the name, though, have a real history.

    Fudge's earliest documented appearance is American and collegiate. Vassar College's own documentary chronicle records that in 1912 Emelyn Hartridge, class of 1892, wrote to the Vassar historian Lucy Maynard Salmon that she had introduced the confection to the college in 1888, having learned of it in Baltimore: "Fudge, as I first knew it, was first made in Baltimore by a cousin of a schoolmate of mine." Hartridge added that it "was sold in 1886 in a grocery store for 40 cents a pound," that she got the recipe from her schoolmate Nannie Hagner, and that "in my first year I made 30 pounds for the Senior Auction, its real introduction to the college." The chronicle also carries a student letter from November 1893 describing "fudges" made with sugar and chocolate, and notes the candy stayed known as "Vassar fudges" for years. Vassar's alumnae quarterly calls the Hartridge letter "the first documented mention of this now classic American candy."

    The peanut half begins with a patent that is, on its own terms, a candy patent. On October 21, 1884, Marcellus G. Edson of Montreal was granted U.S. Patent 306,727 for the "Manufacture of Peanut-Candy." Edson roasted and shelled peanuts, ground them between heated surfaces, and described the result cooling "into a consistency like that of butter, lard, or ointment" — then specified taking "one part of the said peanut-paste and about seven parts of sugar" for sweetmeats and "any form of candy required." Sugar was in the invention from the start.

    What peanut butter legally is came much later. Under the Food and Drug Administration's standard of identity at 21 CFR 164.150, seasoning and stabilizing ingredients may not "in the aggregate exceed 10 percent of the weight of the finished food" — the rule behind the 90-percent-peanuts shorthand — fat may not exceed 55 percent, and "artificial flavorings, artificial sweeteners, chemical preservatives, and color additives are not suitable ingredients in peanut butter." Jars that miss those limits are sold as peanut spread.

    Timeline

    • 21 October 1884 — Marcellus G. Edson of Montreal is granted U.S. Patent 306,727 for the "Manufacture of Peanut-Candy," covering a roasted-peanut paste compounded with sugar.
    • 1886 — Fudge is sold in a Baltimore grocery store at 40 cents a pound, as Emelyn Hartridge recalled it in 1912.
    • 1888 — Hartridge, Vassar class of 1892, makes 30 pounds of fudge for the college's senior auction, which she called its real introduction to Vassar.
    • November 1893 — A Vassar student letter describes "fudges" and its ingredients, the chronicle's earliest contemporaneous mention.
    • 15 March 1977 — 42 FR 14475, the earliest Federal Register citation in the source note to 21 CFR 164.150, the peanut butter standard of identity.

    How to observe

    Read the Edson patent. It is barely more than a page, the prose is plain, and the claims are specific enough to follow: roasted peanuts, heated grinding surfaces, one part paste to seven parts sugar — an everyday food as first described by its inventor.

    Check your own jar against the federal standard. If the label says "spread" rather than "butter," the product is over the 10 percent limit on non-peanut ingredients, carries more than 55 percent fat, or includes something 21 CFR 164.150 excludes. That word is legal, not marketing.

    Make it with a thermometer rather than a clock. Fudge is a crystallization problem, not a baking one: the texture depends on how an oversaturated sugar syrup cools, so the temperature you stop at and how long it sits before beating matter more than the brand of peanut butter.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who invented peanut butter fudge?

    Nobody documented it. Fudge's earliest recorded appearance is at Vassar College in 1888 and ground peanut paste was patented in 1884, but no source establishes who combined them.

    Why is National Peanut Butter Fudge Day on November 20?

    There is no documented reason. No organization, agency or news outlet accounts for the date; it appears only on holiday-listing sites, which is why this page offers no origin story.

    Is it an official holiday?

    No. It has no federal, state or municipal designation, nothing closes, and no sponsoring body claims it.

    What is the difference between peanut butter and peanut spread?

    A legal one. FDA regulation caps non-peanut seasoning and stabilizing ingredients at 10 percent of the finished weight and fat at 55 percent, and excludes artificial flavorings, artificial sweeteners, chemical preservatives and color additives. A product outside those limits must be labeled a spread.

    Did peanut butter start as a health food or a candy?

    The 1884 patent is a candy patent. Edson's claims cover a peanut paste and, separately, that paste "compounded with sugar" for use "in the manufacture of sweetmeats and candy."
