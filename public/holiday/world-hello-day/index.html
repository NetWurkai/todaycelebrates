    World Hello Day falls on November 21 and asks almost nothing of anyone: greet ten people. It was begun in response to the Arab-Israeli war of October 1973, by two brothers who spent their own money on postage, and its organizers say people in 180 countries have marked it since.

    History

    The day's own organizers are strikingly brief about its origin. Worldhelloday.org says only that "World Hello Day was begun in response to the conflict between Egypt and Israel in the Fall of 1973," and that "Anyone can participate in World Hello Day simply by greeting ten people." The site's footer names Brian McCormack, a Ph.D. from Arizona State University, and Michael McCormack of Harvard University — but it describes them as people who "work together to promote this annual global event." It does not say they founded it.

    The founding account comes from elsewhere. A Harvard Crimson profile from June 1999 reports that Michael J. McCormack, Harvard class of 1974, spent the fall of his senior year starting his own holiday: he and his brother Brian "wanted to do something in response to the 1973 Arab-Israeli conflict," the two "spent all their money on postage and sent a letter to as many world leaders as they could find," and in the first year they won the support of 15 countries. The Saturday Evening Post, writing in 2017, puts a figure on that mailing — 1,360 letters in seven languages, sent to world leaders and government officials ahead of a first World Hello Day on November 21.

    The two descriptions of the war itself differ, and the organizers' is the narrower one. Worldhelloday.org names Egypt and Israel; the Saturday Evening Post describes "the armed conflict between Israel and a coalition of Arab states." The U.S. State Department's Office of the Historian records that on October 6, 1973, Egypt and Syria attacked Israeli forces in the Sinai Peninsula and the Golan Heights, and that the fighting ended after the Security Council adopted Resolution 340 on October 25. Neither the organizers nor the Post uses the name the war is usually known by in the United States.

    Timeline

    • October 6, 1973 — Egypt and Syria attack Israeli forces in the Sinai Peninsula and the Golan Heights.
    • October 25, 1973 — The UN Security Council adopts Resolution 340; this ceasefire holds, and Israel accepts it.
    • November 21, 1973 — The first World Hello Day. The McCormack brothers mail 1,360 letters in seven languages to world leaders and officials, and 15 countries back the day that first year.
    • June 1999 — The Harvard Crimson reports that residents of 179 countries have marked the day and that McCormack holds written support from almost 100 authors, entertainers, Nobel Prize winners and world leaders.
    • November 21, 2017 — The Saturday Evening Post calls it the 45th World Hello Day, a count that places the first in 1973; by the same arithmetic, 2026 is the 54th.
    • 2026 — The organizers' site reports 180 countries and 31 Nobel Peace Prize winners among the day's correspondents.

    How to observe

    Greet ten people. That is the organizers' whole instruction, and in more than fifty years they have not added to it. The point, as they put it, is to demonstrate "the importance of personal communication for preserving peace" — the greeting is a gesture toward using words rather than force, not a fundraiser or a hashtag.

    Read the letters. The organizers keep an archive of replies the campaign has drawn over the decades; Queen Elizabeth II, Desmond Tutu and Bill Clinton are the three the site puts on its front page, and it counts 31 Nobel Peace Prize winners among its correspondents.

    Learn a greeting in a language you do not speak. The Crimson reported that McCormack himself could say hello in more than 65 languages, among them Bantu, Inuit and Urdu, which is a more interesting way to spend the day than ten greetings in English.

    And treat the low bar as the design rather than a flaw. An observance that asks for ten hellos cannot be measured, and what the organizers publish is self-reported.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who started World Hello Day?

    Two brothers, Michael and Brian McCormack, in 1973 — though the day's own website does not quite say so. Worldhelloday.org describes the pair as promoting the event rather than founding it. The founding account comes from a 1999 Harvard Crimson profile of Michael McCormack and a 2017 Saturday Evening Post article.

    Why is it on November 21?

    No acceptable source explains the date. The organizers give a reason for the day's existence — the war of autumn 1973 — but none for the day of the month, and neither the Crimson nor the Post asks.

    Is World Hello Day a United Nations observance?

    No. It rests on no resolution and no proclamation, appears on no UN calendar, and is run by the people who started it.

    How many countries observe it?

    The organizers say 180. That number is self-reported and has grown slowly: the Crimson gave 179 in 1999. It counts countries where somebody has marked the day, not governments that endorse it — a separate and much smaller figure, which stood at 15 in the first year.

    What are you actually supposed to do?

    Say hello to ten people. There is nothing else to it, which is the whole idea.
