    National Electric Guitar Day falls on November 27, the day Jimi Hendrix was born in 1942 — though no source documents that the observance was built around that, or documents who built it at all. The instrument's own history is well recorded, and messier than the single-inventor version usually told. The electric guitar was not invented to sound different. It was invented to be louder.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. The instrument is another matter.

    The problem electrification solved was volume. Performance spaces grew, and as the Smithsonian historian Monica M. Smith puts it, "stringed instruments like guitars were hard to hear over other instruments, especially horns." Britannica adds that amplification "made it possible for the guitar to be heard among the other instruments in the big bands," particularly the brass of the swing era. The side effect mattered more: a louder guitar worked as a solo instrument.

    Around 1925 John Dopyera built a guitar with metal resonating cones to amplify it mechanically. Then the electronics arrived, and the two best accounts diverge. The Smithsonian dates the instrument's birth to 1929, when Stromberg-Voisinet of Chicago offered what it calls the first commercially advertised electric guitar, "though it was not a smash hit." Britannica instead puts the first true electric guitar in the early 1930s, from George Beauchamp with Adolph Rickenbacker's help, for the Electro String Instrument Corporation. The claims describe different things — advertised versus workable — so this page keeps both.

    Both agree on what succeeded. The Rickenbacker nicknamed the Frying Pan, invented in 1931, used a pickup made from a pair of horseshoe magnets, and is the Smithsonian's first commercially successful electric guitar. Gibson's 1936 ES-150 carried a bar-shaped pickup nicknamed "The Charlie Christian," after the guitarist credited with introducing the electric guitar solo, who in 1939 stepped out in front of Benny Goodman's band.

    Who built the first solid-body is openly disputed — historians "enjoy debating" it, the Smithsonian says. Britannica credits Les Paul in 1941; the National Museum of American History holds a Slingerland Songster made in or before 1939 that it calls possibly the earliest commercially marketed one. Leo Fender settled the market rather than the argument, as the first to mass-produce and sell a successful solid-body: the Broadcaster, renamed Telecaster after a trademark dispute. Gibson's Ted McCarty dismissed it as a "plank guitar," then answered with the 1952 Les Paul model; the Stratocaster followed in 1954, and a 1958 Les Paul carried the humbucking pickup, built to take in less interference from nearby equipment.

    One date is worth naming rather than averaging: the Smithsonian puts the Broadcaster at 1950, Britannica at 1948. This page follows the Smithsonian, written by a historian at its Lemelson Center for the museum that holds the instruments; the Britannica entry carries no byline or date.

    Timeline

    • 1929 — Stromberg-Voisinet of Chicago offers what the Smithsonian calls the first commercially advertised electric guitar. It is not a success.
    • 1931 — The Rickenbacker "Frying Pan," its pickup made from a pair of horseshoe magnets, becomes the first commercially successful electric guitar.
    • 1936 — Gibson's ES-150 arrives with a bar pickup later nicknamed "The Charlie Christian"; in 1939 Christian steps out in front of Benny Goodman's band.
    • 1950 — Leo Fender's Broadcaster, renamed Telecaster after a trademark dispute, is the first successful mass-produced solid-body electric. Britannica dates it to 1948.
    • 1952-1958 — Gibson answers with the Les Paul model in 1952; the Stratocaster follows in 1954; a 1958 Les Paul carries the new humbucking pickup.

    How to observe

    Go and look at the objects. The National Museum of American History holds the Slingerland Songster made in or before 1939 and the 1954 Stratocaster bearing serial number 0100, and its Invention of the Electric Guitar exhibition comes from the Lemelson Center. A cast-aluminum lap steel beside a Stratocaster collapses twenty years of argument into one glance.

    Then listen to 1939. Charlie Christian in front of Benny Goodman's band is the moment the instrument stopped being a volume fix and became a voice.

    If you own an electric guitar, one piece of this history is audible at home. A single-coil pickup takes in interference from the electrical equipment around it; the humbucking pickup on the 1958 Les Paul was built to reject it. Stand near a dimmer switch with each and the 1958 design explains itself.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Electric Guitar Day?

    November 27, every year.

    Who started National Electric Guitar Day?

    No one that can be documented. No manufacturer, museum, trade body or news outlet claims the day, and no source names a founder or a first year.

    Is the date Jimi Hendrix's birthday?

    It is. Britannica gives his birth as November 27, 1942, in Seattle, Washington, and his death as September 18, 1970, in London. But no source documents that the day was created for that reason, so the overlap is a fact about Hendrix rather than an explanation of the observance.

    Who invented the electric guitar?

    There is no single answer, and the two best sources differ. Britannica names George Beauchamp, with Adolph Rickenbacker's help, in the early 1930s. The Smithsonian dates the instrument to 1929 and the first commercially advertised one to Stromberg-Voisinet of Chicago, and calls the 1931 Rickenbacker "Frying Pan" the first commercially successful one. Who built the first solid-body is disputed too, though Leo Fender was unarguably the first to mass-produce one successfully.
