    National Cake Day falls on November 26, and nobody appears to have asked for it: no bakery trade group, manufacturer, agency or news outlet claims the day, and no source explains the date. The subject is sturdier than the observance. Cake as Americans now bake it — tall, soft, sweet and reliably repeatable — depends on two pieces of industrial chemistry that are younger than the country, and both are well documented.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. What can be sourced is the thing itself, and the word is older than the dessert. Merriam-Webster traces the noun to Middle English, from the Old Norse kaka, akin to the Old High German kuocho, and dates its first known English use to the 13th century. The oldest sense the dictionary records is not a dessert at all but "a breadlike food made from a dough or batter that is usually fried or baked in small flat shapes and is often unleavened." The sweet, raised version — "usually containing flour and sugar and often shortening, eggs, and a raising agent (such as baking powder)" — sits beside it as a separate sense.

    What separated the two was leavening. Yeast had done the work in bread for millennia, but it is slow, and the alternatives were costly: in the 1840s bakers found that cream of tartar would generate carbon dioxide when mixed with baking soda, and American bakers had to import cream of tartar at great expense. In 1854 Eben Norton Horsford, who had held the Rumford Professorship at Harvard since 1847, began looking for a domestic substitute. He proposed monocalcium phosphate, made by treating animal bones with sulfuric acid, and in 1856 received a patent for the process of making it. He built the Rumford Chemical Works in East Providence, Rhode Island, to manufacture it, and in an 1861 tract on bread making argued that chemical leavening worked faster than yeast and asked less skill of the home baker. He took out thirty patents in all. The American Chemical Society named the development of baking powder a National Historic Chemical Landmark in 2006.

    The second shift was the box, and it took longer to sell than to invent. In 1933 P. Duff and Sons, a Pittsburgh molasses company, patented the first cake mix: dehydrated molasses blended with dried flour, sugar and eggs, needing only water and an oven to yield gingerbread. John D. Duff patented a version requiring fresh eggs in 1935. By 1947 more than 200 companies were making mixes for regional distribution and buyers were unconvinced — they were expensive and the results were inconsistent. General Mills spent four years on the problem and released its Betty Crocker Ginger Cake mix in 1947. Sales tripled between January 1947 and August 1948, then flattened. The company hired the market researcher Ernest Dichter, who concluded after interviewing groups of women that the mixes were so simple they felt self-indulgent; General Mills took the dried eggs out so that bakers had to add their own, and sales improved. Devil's Food and Party Cake followed in 1949, yellow and white in 1952. A Gallup poll in 1953 ranked cake the second "real test of a woman's ability to cook," behind apple pie.

    Timeline

    • 13th century — The earliest known English use of "cake," in the sense of a small, flat, often unleavened breadlike food.
    • 1840s — Bakers find that cream of tartar generates carbon dioxide with baking soda; American bakers import it at great expense.
    • 1856 — Eben Norton Horsford patents the process for making monocalcium phosphate, the acid in modern baking powder, and builds the Rumford Chemical Works to produce it.
    • 1933 — P. Duff and Sons of Pittsburgh patents the first cake mix; a version needing fresh eggs follows in 1935.
    • 1947 — General Mills releases Betty Crocker Ginger Cake after four years of development, into a market already holding more than 200 regional mix makers.
    • 2006 — The American Chemical Society names the development of baking powder a National Historic Chemical Landmark.

    How to observe

    Read the label on your baking powder. If the ingredient list includes monocalcium phosphate, that is the compound Horsford patented in 1856, still in the can and still doing the job he designed it for.

    Bake one recipe twice, once from a mix and once from scratch. The mix was deliberately re-engineered to make you crack an egg into it, which makes the comparison a reasonable test of whether Dichter was right about what home bakers want from the work.

    Read a dated account rather than a listicle. MNopedia, the Minnesota Historical Society's encyclopedia, keeps a year-by-year chronology of the Betty Crocker mixes — flavors, research, launch dates — which is more specific than almost anything written about cake online, and free.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Cake Day?

    November 26, every year.

    Who started National Cake Day?

    No one that can be documented. No baker, trade association, manufacturer, agency or news outlet claims the day, and no source names a founder or a first year. The origin of this observance is not well documented.

    What is the difference between cake and bread?

    Less than the modern dessert suggests. Merriam-Webster's oldest sense of "cake" is a breadlike food, often unleavened, baked or fried in small flat shapes; the sweet sense, with sugar, fat and a raising agent, is listed separately. In practice the raising agent is what split them, and chemical leavening only arrived in the middle of the 19th century.

    Who invented baking powder?

    Modern baking powder came out of work by Eben Norton Horsford, a Harvard chemist, who was looking for a domestic replacement for imported cream of tartar. He began in 1854 and patented the process for making monocalcium phosphate in 1856.

    Why is boxed cake mix so common in American home baking?

    It was patented in 1933 and took about two decades to win people over. By 1947 more than 200 companies made mixes, and General Mills' Betty Crocker line, launched that year, is credited with making them nationally popular in the early 1950s.
