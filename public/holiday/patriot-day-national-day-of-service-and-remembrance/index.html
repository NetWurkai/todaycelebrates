    Patriot Day — National Day of Service & Remembrance

    Patriot Day falls on September 11 and honors the 2,977 people killed in the terrorist attacks of 2001. Congress designated it by joint resolution in December of that year, which means the first Patriot Day was September 11, 2002. It is a day of remembrance and, since 2009, of service; it is not a holiday, and nothing closes.

    History

    The designation came quickly. House Joint Resolution 71 passed the House on October 25, 2001 and the Senate on November 30, and was approved on December 18 as Public Law 107-89. Its operative language, now at 36 U.S.C. 144, is four words: "September 11 is Patriot Day." The rest of the statute asks the president to issue a proclamation each year calling on governments and the public to observe the day, on federal agencies to fly the flag at half-staff, and on Americans to observe a moment of silence. The statute names no time for that silence.

    An earlier proclamation is sometimes mistaken for the first Patriot Day. Proclamation 7462, signed September 13, 2001, designated Friday, September 14 as a National Day of Prayer and Remembrance, with "noontime memorial services, the ringing of bells at that hour, and evening candlelight remembrance vigils." That was a single day, not an annual observance.

    The service half of the day arrived eight years later, and the law behind it is usually described wrongly. The Edward M. Kennedy Serve America Act, Public Law 111-13 of April 21, 2009, does not amend the Patriot Day statute and does not contain the word "Patriot" anywhere. What it did was add a provision to the national service laws authorizing the federal service agency, now AmeriCorps, to organize activities and make grants for "the September 11th National Day of Service and Remembrance" — an observance the 2009 proclamation credited to the victims' families. So "Patriot Day and National Day of Service and Remembrance" is the title of a presidential proclamation, not the day's name in statute — and administrations have used that longer title inconsistently.

    The 8:46 a.m. Eastern moment of silence also comes from the proclamations rather than the law; it marks the time American Airlines Flight 11 struck the North Tower. The 2002 proclamation asked for silence "beginning at 8:46 a.m. eastern daylight time, or another appropriate commemorative time"; the 2009 one dropped the alternative, and recent proclamations have directed half-staff without naming a time at all.

    Timeline

    • 2001 — Public Law 107-89, approved December 18, designates September 11 as Patriot Day; it is codified at 36 U.S.C. 144.
    • 2002 — The first Patriot Day is observed on September 11 under Proclamation 7590.
    • 2008 — The Pentagon Memorial, a bench for each of the 184 people killed there, is dedicated on September 11.
    • 2009 — The Serve America Act authorizes federal support for a September 11 National Day of Service and Remembrance.
    • 2011 — The 9/11 Memorial is dedicated on the tenth anniversary and opens to the public the next day.

    How to observe

    The 9/11 Memorial maintains a searchable directory of every name inscribed on the bronze parapets, which locates a person by panel. Reading one entry, rather than a total, is the closest thing to the point of the day.

    For the service half, AmeriCorps runs a volunteer search for projects tied to September 11, and the nonprofit 9/11 Day organizes meal-packing events that produced more than 6.5 million meals across eighteen cities in 2023. If you want primary material, the Library of Congress's September 11, 2001 Documentary Project holds oral histories recorded in the weeks after the attacks, with roughly 200 interviews online. The Flight 93 National Memorial near Shanksville and the Pentagon Memorial, with its 184 benches, are both open to visitors.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Patriot Day a federal holiday?

    No. The eleven legal public holidays are listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103, and Patriot Day is not among them. It sits in Title 36, the patriotic observances title, so no federal leave attaches.

    How many people were killed?

    The 9/11 Memorial counts 2,977 people killed at the three attack sites on September 11, 2001: 2,753 in New York, 184 at the Pentagon, and 40 aboard Flight 93. That figure counts victims and excludes the nineteen hijackers. The memorial bears 2,983 names, because it also honors the six people killed in the 1993 World Trade Center bombing. Among the 2,977 were 441 first responders from more than thirty agencies, including 343 New York City firefighters, 37 Port Authority police officers and 23 New York City police officers.

    How many have died since of 9/11-related illness?

    There is no official count. The federal World Trade Center Health Program states plainly that it does not track cause of death for its members, so its figures cannot answer the question. The Justice Department's Victim Compensation Fund reported 8,996 claims filed on behalf of people who had died as of December 31, 2025, but a claim is not a certified cause of death.

    Is Patriot Day the same as Patriots' Day?

    No. Patriots' Day is a state holiday in Massachusetts and Maine observed on the third Monday in April, commemorating the battles of Lexington and Concord in 1775.

    What time is the moment of silence?

    Proclamations have commonly set it at 8:46 a.m. Eastern, the moment of the first impact, though the statute itself names no time and recent proclamations have directed only that flags fly at half-staff.
