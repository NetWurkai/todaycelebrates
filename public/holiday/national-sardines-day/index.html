    National Sardines Day falls on November 24, and no one appears to claim it: no fishery agency, processor or trade body designates the date, and nothing explains the choice of day. The fish is better documented than the holiday. A sardine is not one species but a label shared by more than twenty, and both American sardine industries — Maine's canneries and the Pacific fishery — are now gone or closed.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. The date appears on aggregator calendars, which are not sources; no government page, industry body, cannery or news report consulted for this page names a founder or a year.

    The fish is a different matter, starting with the word. The Codex Alimentarius standard for canned sardines, CODEX STAN 94-1981, lists twenty-three species that may be packed as sardines, among them Atlantic herring, European sprat and three anchovies. Its labeling clause reserves the plain word: "Sardines" is for Sardina pilchardus alone, and everything else must be sold as "X sardines," where X names a country, a region, the species or its common name. A can on an American shelf is almost always one of those qualified names.

    The eastern industry began in Eastport, Maine, in the late 1800s, and more than 400 sardine factories opened and closed along the state's coast after that. The fish in those cans was herring. Ronnie Peabody, who runs the Maine Coast Sardine History Museum in Jonesport, told the Bangor Daily News in 2010 that sardines were the state's largest industry in the early 1950s, supporting nearly 6,000 jobs. Connors Bros. bought the four Stinson Seafood canneries in 1999; Belfast and Lubec closed within a few years, Bath in 2005, and on April 14, 2010 the Prospect Harbor plant packed the last sardine canned in the country, taking 130 jobs with it.

    The western story is a fishery rather than a factory. Created in the 1920s, the Pacific sardine fishery peaked in the 1930s as the largest on the North American Pacific coast. CalCOFI, the program founded in 1949 to study the collapse that followed, records landings peaking in 1936-37 at 791,334 tons, 726,124 of them in California; Norman Scofield had recommended an annual cap of 200,000 tons in 1931 and Frances Clark 250,000 in 1939, and landings ran well past both. By 1954-56 the coastwide catch was about 80,000 tons, and by 1965-67 about 20,000. The cause was argued for decades, and CalCOFI's own history says the current view holds environment, fishing pressure and predation together.

    It has not come back. NOAA Fisheries states that "commercial fishing for Pacific sardines is prohibited because the population is estimated to be below a precautionary level set by managers" — 150,000 metric tons. The directed fishery closed in 2015, the stock was assessed as overfished in 2025, and the rebuilding plan targets 2035. In 2014, the last year of fishing, more than 23,000 metric tons were landed, worth $8.85 million.

    Timeline

    • 1931 and 1939 — Norman Scofield recommends capping the Pacific catch at 200,000 tons a year, Frances Clark at 250,000. Neither cap is adopted.
    • 1936-37 — Pacific sardine landings peak at 791,334 tons, 726,124 of them in California.
    • 1949 — CalCOFI is formed to study the collapse. By 1965-67 landings are down to about 20,000 tons.
    • April 14, 2010 — Stinson Seafood in Prospect Harbor, Maine, the country's last sardine cannery, packs its final fish; 130 jobs go with it.
    • 2015 — The directed Pacific sardine fishery closes. It has not reopened, and the stock was assessed overfished in 2025.

    How to observe

    Read the can. The Codex rule puts the species, and usually the country, on the label, so the tin will say whether you are eating Sardina pilchardus from Portugal, Atlantic herring, a Sardinella or an anchovy. Buying two different tins and tasting them against each other is the fastest way to learn that "sardine" is a category rather than a fish.

    Then eat them as they were eaten before they became emergency food. The food historian Sandy Oliver, quoted in that 2010 Bangor Daily News piece, notes that sardines were a mid-to-late-1800s luxury sold with their own small dishes and forks, and that sardines and crackers sat on bars before Prohibition, where popcorn and nuts sit now. The Maine Coast Sardine History Museum in Jonesport, opened in 2001, keeps the trade's own record; confirm hours before driving up the coast.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is a sardine a single species of fish?

    No. The Codex standard lists twenty-three species that may be sold as sardines, including Atlantic herring, European sprat and three anchovies. Only Sardina pilchardus may be labeled plain "Sardines"; the rest need a qualifier, as in "Pacific sardines."

    Are sardines still canned in the United States?

    Not according to the most recent reporting found for this page. Stinson Seafood in Prospect Harbor, Maine, the nation's last sardine cannery, packed its final fish on April 14, 2010, and no acceptable source consulted records one opening since.

    Are sardines high in mercury?

    No. In the FDA and EPA advice on eating fish, sardines are a "Best Choice" — two to three servings a week during pregnancy and breastfeeding, two for children — and are absent from the agencies' highest-mercury list.

    Why is National Sardines Day on November 24?

    No source consulted answers that: no government agency, trade body or news outlet designates the day or explains the date.
