    National Greek Yogurt Day falls on November 9, with no traceable founder or first observance behind it. The product it marks sits in a stranger position than the day does. The Food and Drug Administration has observed that thicker Greek-style yogurt became "as popular as regular yogurt" as the category grew — and yet "Greek yogurt" still has no legal definition in the United States, which is why in January 2025 the agency formally asked the industry to explain how the stuff is actually made.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding organization, government page or news account establishes who created National Greek Yogurt Day or when it was first marked; the date appears to circulate through daily-holiday aggregators alone. The regulatory history of the product, though, is documented in detail.

    Yogurt in the United States is governed by a federal standard of identity at 21 CFR 131.200. There is no equivalent standard for the thicker, higher-protein product sold as Greek yogurt. In a notice published January 15, 2025, the agency wrote: "There is not a separate SOI for high-protein yogurt, Greek yogurt, or Greek-style yogurt." It was a request for information, docket FDA-2024-N-5716, asking manufacturers to describe the processes and ingredients they use, on the stated ground that the existing standard "may not align with certain manufacturing processes and ingredients used to concentrate protein."

    That notice is also the clearest official description of how the product is made, and the answer is that there is more than one way. One method is to concentrate yogurt after culturing to remove liquid whey by straining, which FDA notes "can be done in a traditional way using cheesecloth, but is more commonly done commercially using a centrifugal separator." A second adds milk-protein ingredients before culturing. A third concentrates milk protein by membrane ultrafiltration. All three can reach the shelf under the same name. FDA also records granting Chobani a temporary permit in March 2023 to market-test yogurt made with ultrafiltered nonfat milk as a basic dairy ingredient.

    The gap is not new: FDA says manufacturers argued for an additional standard for strained, high-protein yogurt at a public meeting in 2019 and again in a reopened comment period in 2020. The agency's broader yogurt rulemaking, published June 11, 2021 and effective that July, revoked the separate standards for lowfat and nonfat yogurt and amended the yogurt standard in numerous respects. It did not create a Greek yogurt standard. Explaining the change, FDA noted that optional dairy protein ingredients let manufacturers reach the protein content Greek-style yogurt needs before fermentation, "without the production of the undesirable acid whey that is potentially a disposal problem."

    Timeline

    • 2019 and 2020 — Yogurt manufacturers ask the FDA, at a public meeting and then in a reopened comment period, for an additional standard of identity for strained, high-protein yogurt.
    • June 11, 2021 — The FDA publishes its final rule revoking the lowfat and nonfat yogurt standards and amending the yogurt standard at 21 CFR 131.200, effective July 12, 2021, compliance date January 1, 2024. No Greek yogurt standard is created.
    • March 2023 — The FDA grants Chobani a temporary permit to market-test yogurt made with ultrafiltered nonfat milk as a basic dairy ingredient.
    • January 15, 2025 — The FDA publishes a request for information on high-protein, Greek and Greek-style yogurt, stating that no separate standard of identity exists for it.
    • April 15, 2025 — The comment period on that request closes.

    How to observe

    Read the ingredient list rather than the front of the tub. With no standard of identity for Greek yogurt, two containers carrying the name can be made by materially different processes, and the ingredients are where that shows. Milk and live cultures alone generally indicates a strained product; added milk protein concentrate or whey protein indicates protein built in rather than strained out.

    Then compare protein and sugar across two brands at the same fat level and price. Protein density is the category's commercial logic, and the spread between brands is wider than the packaging implies.

    The FDA's 2025 request for information is short and plainly written, and asks exactly what a curious reader would: what the methods are, how each changes the product, and what the result ought legally to be called. The docket and its comments are public.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Greek Yogurt Day?

    November 9, every year.

    Who started National Greek Yogurt Day?

    No reliable source names a founder, sponsoring organization or first observance. Any origin story attached to this day is unsourced.

    Is "Greek yogurt" a legally defined term in the United States?

    No. The FDA stated in January 2025 that "there is not a separate SOI for high-protein yogurt, Greek yogurt, or Greek-style yogurt" — SOI meaning standard of identity. Yogurt generally is defined at 21 CFR 131.200.

    How is Greek yogurt made?

    By more than one method, according to the FDA: straining cultured yogurt to remove liquid whey, traditionally with cheesecloth and commercially with a centrifugal separator; adding milk-protein ingredients before culturing; or concentrating milk protein by ultrafiltration.

    What is acid whey?

    It is the liquid drawn off when yogurt is strained. Explaining its 2021 rule, the FDA called it "undesirable acid whey that is potentially a disposal problem" — part of why some manufacturers build protein in before fermentation instead.
