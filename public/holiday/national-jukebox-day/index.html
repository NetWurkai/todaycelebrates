    National Jukebox Day falls on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, which puts it on November 25 in 2026. It is a trade promotion rather than a civic observance: a jukebox company declared it in 2016 and picked the date because the night before Thanksgiving is the busiest bar night of the year. The machine it points back to is far better documented than the day itself.

    History

    The day is a marketing creation. On November 21, 2016, the amusement-trade magazine RePlay reported that TouchTunes, which operates digital jukeboxes in bars, had declared that Wednesday, November 23, to be National Jukebox Day, citing the 1889 anniversary of the first coin-operated phonograph. Its marketing and programming head, Vicki Saunders, gave the commercial reason without dressing it up: the day before Thanksgiving is traditionally the busiest bar night of the year. The date has since traveled with Thanksgiving rather than staying on the 23rd. Vending Times reported the Amusement & Music Operators Association running a 2021 promotion through National Jukebox Day on November 24, "the day before Thanksgiving," and AMI Entertainment's 2024 giveaway called it the Wednesday before Thanksgiving every year. No agency, nonprofit or founding body stands behind it.

    The anniversary underneath it is real. Britannica records that on November 23, 1889, Louis Glass of the Pacific Phonograph Company and his partner William S. Arnold installed a coin-operated Edison Class M phonograph at the Palais Royal Saloon in San Francisco. There was no speaker and no way to choose a song: four listeners, each having paid a nickel, held stethoscope-style tubes to their ears and all heard the same wax cylinder. They filed a patent on December 18 for the mechanism controlling access to the tubes, and it was granted the following May as number 428,750. At an industry convention that same month, Glass reported that the saloon machine had collected $1,035.25 since its debut, and that fifteen machines, in other saloons and on the Oakland-San Francisco ferry, had taken $4,019 between them. "There is money in the nickel-in-the-slot phonograph," he told his fellow operators.

    Nobody called it a jukebox. Merriam-Webster records the word's first known use in 1939 and derives it from the dialect word jukehouse, from juke, a term of Atlantic Creole origin related to Jamaican English juk, to poke or stab. The machine most people picture came in between: the Smithsonian's finding aid for the Wurlitzer Company Records notes that the firm, founded in Cincinnati in 1856, had given up automatic musical instruments through the 1920s and 1930s "until the manufacture of the first jukebox in 1934." Its North Tonawanda plant built them until 1974.

    Timeline

    • November 23, 1889 — Louis Glass and William S. Arnold install a coin-operated Edison Class M phonograph at the Palais Royal Saloon in San Francisco: four listening tubes, a nickel each, no speaker.
    • May 1890 — Glass tells a convention the saloon machine has collected $1,035.25 and fifteen machines $4,019 between them; the patent filed that December 18 is granted as number 428,750.
    • 1934 — Wurlitzer, in business since 1856 and out of the automatic-instrument trade through the Depression, manufactures its first jukebox.
    • 1939 — "Jukebox" is recorded in print for the first time.
    • 1974 — Wurlitzer's North Tonawanda plant stops making jukeboxes after four decades.
    • 2016 — TouchTunes declares November 23 National Jukebox Day; the observance settles on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving.

    How to observe

    The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds most of a jukebox exhibit in its online collection records, free to read. Among the 174 records its catalog returns for "jukebox" are a Wurlitzer Model 1100 from about 1950 with its wall selector unit, a chrome box of twenty-four push buttons built for a diner booth; a Scopitone film jukebox from about 1966; the jukebox from Tootsie's Orchid Lounge, where the museum's record says hopeful musicians could get their demo 45s played; and Wurlitzer's own photographs of workers assembling jukeboxes at North Tonawanda in 1934-35 and 1939.

    If you would rather do something with the evening, the operators' pitch doubles as the honest instruction: find a bar with a working machine and pay for a few songs. Note that the date moves with Thanksgiving rather than sitting on a fixed November day.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Jukebox Day?

    The Wednesday before Thanksgiving, which is November 25 in 2026. It is not a fixed date: trade coverage puts it on November 23 in 2016, November 24 in 2021 and November 26 in 2025, each the day before Thanksgiving.

    Who created National Jukebox Day?

    TouchTunes, which operates digital jukeboxes in bars, declared it in 2016, saying the day before Thanksgiving is traditionally the busiest bar night of the year. AMI Entertainment and the Amusement & Music Operators Association have run promotions around it since.

    Was the 1889 machine really a jukebox?

    Not in the sense the word carries now. It had no speaker and no way to pick a song: four people listened through tubes to the same wax cylinder. Britannica notes that the word arrived decades later, once coin-operated phonographs had selectable records and amplification.

    Where does the word "jukebox" come from?

    Merriam-Webster records its first known use in 1939 and derives it from the dialect word jukehouse, from juke, a word of Atlantic Creole origin related to Jamaican English juk, to poke or stab.

    Does any official body designate the day?

    No. There is no proclamation, agency or nonprofit behind it and no founding document to read: it exists in jukebox-industry promotions and the trade press that covers them.
