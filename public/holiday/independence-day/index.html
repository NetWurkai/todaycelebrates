    Independence Day

    Send viaEmailTextFacebookXWhatsApp
    Date
    Sunday, July 4, 2027
    Recurs
    Every year on July 4
    Category
    Celebration

    Independence Day marks July 4, 1776, the day the Second Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence. It does not mark the vote for independence, which came two days earlier, or the signing, which mostly happened a month later. John Adams, who was in the room, expected Americans to celebrate July 2 forever.

    History

    The sequence is documented in detail by the National Archives, which holds the engrossed Declaration. Richard Henry Lee of Virginia put the motion on the floor in Philadelphia on June 7, 1776, calling for the colonies to be "free and independent States." On June 11 Congress postponed consideration, seven colonies to five with New York abstaining, and recessed for three weeks — but first it appointed a Committee of Five to draft the statement of the case: John Adams, Roger Sherman, Benjamin Franklin, Robert R. Livingston, and Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson wrote the initial draft.

    Congress adopted the Lee Resolution on July 2, 1776, twelve of the thirteen colonies in favor and New York not voting. That was the decision. July 4 is the day Congress finished revising Jefferson's text and adopted the Declaration itself, late that morning; John Dunlap printed the first copies that night. On July 19 Congress ordered the document engrossed on parchment and every member to sign it, and the signing began at the table on August 2, Hancock first. Eventually 56 delegates signed, not all of them present that day. The National Archives is explicit that the familiar picture of all the delegates signing on July 4 is a misconception.

    Adams had no doubt which date would matter, and he was wrong. Writing to Abigail on July 3, 1776 — the letter is in the Adams Family Papers at the Massachusetts Historical Society — he said the Second Day of July "will be celebrated, by succeeding Generations, as the great anniversary Festival," a "Day of Deliverance" to be "solemnized with Pomp and Parade, with Shews, Games, Sports, Guns, Bells, Bonfires and Illuminations." He described the festivities almost exactly and missed the date by two days.

    The holiday itself took decades to settle. The Library of Congress notes that observing Independence Day became commonplace only after the War of 1812. Congress made it a federal holiday on June 28, 1870, for the District of Columbia, alongside New Year's Day, Christmas, and Thanksgiving — the first four. It now sits among the eleven legal public holidays in 5 U.S.C. 6103.

    The document's central contradiction was named early, by the people it excluded. On July 5, 1852, at Corinthian Hall in Rochester, New York, Frederick Douglass gave the keynote at an Independence Day celebration and told the room: "This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn."

    Timeline

    • June 7, 1776 — Richard Henry Lee presents his resolution in Philadelphia calling for the colonies to be "free and independent States."
    • June 11, 1776 — Congress postpones the resolution, seven colonies to five with New York abstaining, and appoints the Committee of Five to draft a statement of the case.
    • July 2, 1776 — Congress adopts the Lee Resolution, twelve colonies in favor and New York not voting. This is the vote for independence.
    • July 4, 1776 — Congress finishes its revisions and adopts the Declaration of Independence; John Dunlap prints the first broadsides that night.
    • August 2, 1776 — The engrossed Declaration is signed at the table, Hancock first; 56 delegates sign in all, not all of them present that day.
    • June 28, 1870 — Congress makes Independence Day a federal holiday in the District of Columbia, among the first four.

    How to observe

    Read the Declaration to the end. Nearly everyone knows the second paragraph; the bulk of it is a list of grievances against the Crown, and the closing lines carry the names of men putting their signatures to treason — which is what the August 2 signing was.

    Read Douglass's 1852 address alongside it. It is one of the sharpest things ever written about the Declaration, by someone it did not cover.

    Check when your day off actually falls. July 4, 2027 is a Sunday, so the federal holiday that year is Monday, July 5.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Independence Day a federal holiday?

    Yes, and one of the original four. Congress made it a holiday in the District of Columbia on June 28, 1870, alongside New Year's Day, Christmas, and Thanksgiving. It is now among the eleven legal public holidays listed in 5 U.S.C. 6103.

    What happens when July 4 falls on a weekend?

    Federal employees observe it on the nearest weekday: the preceding Friday when July 4 is a Saturday, and the following Monday when it is a Sunday. July 4, 2027 falls on a Sunday, so the federal holiday is Monday, July 5, 2027.

    Did the Founders sign the Declaration on July 4, 1776?

    Mostly no. Congress adopted the text on July 4, but it did not order the document engrossed on parchment until July 19, and the signing began on August 2. Of the 56 eventual signers, not all were even present that day. The National Archives calls the July 4 signing a misconception.

    Why isn't Independence Day on July 2?

    Because the holiday follows the Declaration's adoption rather than the vote for independence. Congress voted to separate from Britain on July 2, 1776 and adopted the Declaration on July 4. Adams expected July 2 to be the anniversary, and wrote to Abigail on July 3 saying exactly that.
