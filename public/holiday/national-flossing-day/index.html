    National Flossing Day falls on November 27, and nobody appears to be claiming it: no dentist, dental association, manufacturer or agency is documented as having started it, and no source explains the date. The subject underneath is both well documented and genuinely contested. Flossing is one of the most repeated pieces of health advice in the country, and the one the federal government quietly dropped from its dietary guidelines in 2016.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. What can be sourced is the practice, which is about two centuries old.

    In 1819 Levi Spear Parmly, a dentist in New Orleans, published A Practical Guide to the Management of the Teeth and described the device he is generally credited with: a waxed silken thread. As TIME quotes him, care should be taken "to pass the waxed silk in the interstices, and round the necks of the teeth." Within a century, TIME notes, the idea had become dental common practice. Silk held until the Second World War cut American supplies and nylon replaced it, a shift TIME attributes in particular to the dentist Charles C. Bass.

    The modern argument began in August 2016, when Associated Press reporters pushed the federal government for the evidence behind flossing and, as NPR reported, "there wasn't much." Flossing had quietly vanished from the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, the five-yearly publication of the Departments of Health and Human Services and Agriculture; in the edition current when NPR ran the story, "the word floss doesn't show up once." A federal recommendation had first appeared in a surgeon general's report in 1979. HHS said only that neither its 2010 nor its 2015 advisory committee had reviewed brushing and flossing, so the authors "decided not to carry forward" that material, and that they "were not implying that this is not an important oral hygiene practice."

    The evidence is thin, which is not the same as negative. A 2011 Cochrane review of 12 flossing studies found they "were of poor quality and conclusions must be viewed as unreliable," while finding some sign that flossing plus brushing reduced gingivitis. The 2019 Cochrane review the ADA cites graded its evidence low to very low certainty, suggesting floss or interdental brushes added to brushing may reduce gingivitis or plaque, and that the brushes may work better than floss. A 2006 meta-analysis the ADA also cites found professional flossing five days a week significantly reduced cavities between teeth, while self-flossing did not. The ADA has not moved: its guidance, last updated in August 2026, calls an interdental cleaner "an essential part of taking care of teeth and gums."

    Timeline

    • 1819 — Levi Spear Parmly of New Orleans publishes A Practical Guide to the Management of the Teeth, describing the waxed silken thread he is credited with devising.
    • 1979 — A federal recommendation in favor of flossing appears in a surgeon general's report, and later reaches the national dietary guidelines.
    • 2011 — A Cochrane review of 12 flossing studies finds them of poor quality and its own conclusions unreliable, while reporting some reduction in gingivitis.
    • August 2016 — The Associated Press presses the government for evidence; the word does not appear in the current Dietary Guidelines, and HHS says its advisory committees never reviewed the question.
    • 2019 — A Cochrane review grades the evidence low to very low certainty and suggests interdental brushes may be more effective than floss.

    How to observe

    Clean between your teeth once, properly, and stop arguing about the schedule. The ADA recommends it once a day and says the best time is whichever fits your routine; the before-or-after-brushing debate is not one it adjudicates. It does say that "it's generally not what type of floss is used, but how and when it's used," and that waxed and unwaxed floss are equally effective.

    The more useful thing this day can do is change the tool. The ADA's category is interdental cleaners, not floss specifically: small brushes, wooden or plastic picks, water flossers. It notes that floss suits tight spaces while interproximal brushes suit more open ones and people with periodontitis, and the 2019 review it cites suggests the brushes may be more effective. If string floss has always been a losing battle, that is a reason to change device rather than give up.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Flossing Day?

    November 27.

    Who started National Flossing Day?

    No one that can be documented. No dentist, dental association, manufacturer, agency or news outlet claims the day, and no source names a founder or a first year.

    Did the US government stop recommending flossing?

    It stopped printing the recommendation. NPR reported in August 2016 that the word did not appear in the then-current Dietary Guidelines for Americans. HHS said its advisory committees had never reviewed the question, so the material was not carried forward, and that this did not imply flossing was unimportant.

    Does flossing actually work?

    The studies are weak rather than negative. Cochrane reviews in 2011 and 2019 both found low-quality evidence, with some sign that cleaning between the teeth plus brushing reduces gingivitis or plaque more than brushing alone.

    Does the American Dental Association still recommend flossing?

    Yes. The ADA calls an interdental cleaner "an essential part of taking care of teeth and gums" and recommends brushing twice a day and cleaning between the teeth once a day — with floss or another interdental cleaner.
