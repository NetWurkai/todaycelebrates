    The federal holiday on the third Monday in January is, in the statute, the Birthday of Martin Luther King, Jr. — though almost everyone says Martin Luther King, Jr. Day. It took fifteen years to create: a bill was introduced four days after King was killed in 1968 and died without a vote, and the law establishing the holiday was not signed until November 2, 1983. Since 1994 federal law has also pointed the day toward community service, which makes it one of the few American holidays with an instruction attached.

    History

    Representative John Conyers of Michigan introduced the first bill to make King's birthday a national holiday four days after the assassination, in April 1968. Congress took no action. The campaign that eventually won ran another fifteen years and was fought in public more than in committee: Stevie Wonder's 1980 song "Happy Birthday" became its anthem, Coretta Scott King testified repeatedly, millions signed petitions, and marches in Washington in 1982 and 1983 kept the pressure on.

    The bill passed both houses in 1983. President Ronald Reagan signed Public Law 98-144 on November 2, 1983, amending section 6103(a) of title 5 of the United States Code to insert a new line immediately below New Year's Day: the Birthday of Martin Luther King, Jr., the third Monday in January. The law deliberately delayed itself — the amendment took effect on January 1, 1986, more than two years after enactment. In August 1984 Reagan established a commission to prepare the first observance, and he proclaimed Monday, January 20, 1986 as the first Martin Luther King, Jr. Day.

    The states moved at their own pace. South Carolina did not make the third Monday in January a regular state holiday — rather than an optional one state employees could take in place of a Confederate holiday — until an act ratified on April 26, 2000.

    The service dimension came later still. On August 23, 1994, President Bill Clinton signed the King Holiday and Service Act, authorizing the Corporation for National and Community Service, in consultation with the Martin Luther King, Jr. Federal Holiday Commission, to fund service projects on the holiday reflecting King's legacy — among them "cooperation and understanding among racial and ethnic groups, nonviolent conflict resolution, equal economic and educational opportunities, and social justice."

    Timeline

    • April 1968 — Four days after King's assassination, Rep. John Conyers of Michigan introduces the first bill to make his birthday a national holiday. Congress takes no action.
    • 1980 — Stevie Wonder releases "Happy Birthday," which becomes the campaign's rallying song; marches in Washington in 1982 and 1983 build on it.
    • November 2, 1983 — President Ronald Reagan signs Public Law 98-144, adding the Birthday of Martin Luther King, Jr. to 5 U.S.C. 6103 as the third Monday in January, effective January 1, 1986.
    • August 27, 1984 — Reagan establishes a commission to prepare the first observance of the new holiday.
    • January 20, 1986 — The first Martin Luther King, Jr. Day is observed, two days after Reagan's proclamation naming the date.
    • August 23, 1994 — President Bill Clinton signs the King Holiday and Service Act, authorizing federal grants for service projects on the holiday.

    How to observe

    Read King himself rather than quotations of him. The long pieces — the letter from the Birmingham jail, the later speeches on poverty and war — are the ones holiday programming skips in favor of four lines from 1963.

    Serve, if there is something near you worth doing. The 1994 act built grant-funded projects into the day, but almost all of it is organized locally — a city volunteer office, a school district, a congregation, a food bank. Listings go up in the first week of January and the useful ones fill fast.

    Visit Martin Luther King, Jr. National Historical Park in Atlanta. It holds King's birth home, the historic Ebenezer Baptist Church where he and his father preached, and the tomb of Dr. and Mrs. King on a brick island in a reflecting pool. Ranger-led programs are free. Birth home tours take fifteen people at a time, hourly from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and you register in person at Historic Fire Station No. 6 — so arrive early.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the official name of the holiday?

    The Birthday of Martin Luther King, Jr. That is how 5 U.S.C. 6103 lists it. "Martin Luther King, Jr. Day" is the common name and the one Reagan used in the 1986 proclamation.

    Is the holiday on King's actual birthday?

    No. King was born on January 15, 1929. The holiday is the third Monday in January, which can only fall between January 15 and January 21, so it lands on his birthday only in years when January 15 happens to be a Monday.

    Why did it take fifteen years?

    Conyers' 1968 bill died without action and no measure cleared both houses until 1983. The White House's own account credits public pressure rather than legislative maneuvering: Stevie Wonder's "Happy Birthday," Coretta Scott King's repeated testimony, millions of petition signatures, and the 1982 and 1983 marches in Washington.

    Is it a day off or a day of service?

    Both, legally. It is a federal legal public holiday, so federal offices close. The King Holiday and Service Act of 1994 separately authorized federal grants for service projects on the day, which is why much of what happens is volunteer work rather than ceremony.

    Do all states observe it?

    It is a federal holiday, so federal offices close everywhere. State practice took much longer to converge: South Carolina made the third Monday in January a regular rather than optional state holiday only in 2000, by an act ratified that April.
