    Ash Wednesday opens Lent, the forty-day season of prayer, fasting and almsgiving that ends at sundown on Holy Thursday. It is the day Catholics and, increasingly, Protestants receive a mark of ash on the forehead — ash made by burning the palms blessed on the previous year's Palm Sunday. In the United States it is not a holy day of obligation, which makes the size of the crowds it draws one of the more interesting things about it.

    History

    Ashes as a sign of repentance are older than the day named for them. At Rome, Christians guilty of grave sin performed public penance through Lent: barred from Communion, dressed in sackcloth, sprinkled with ashes and kept apart until they were reconciled, a discipline Britannica traces back to Tertullian around 200. Public penance died out between roughly the eighth and tenth centuries, and as it went the ashes moved outward from the penitents to everyone. The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia says the distribution of ashes to all the faithful arose in imitation of the penitents' rite.

    The popular version of what happened next is wrong, and the correction is the most useful thing on this page. Nearly every holiday roundup credits the Synod of Benevento in 1091, often Pope Urban II personally, with extending ashes to the whole congregation. The Catholic Encyclopedia, which actually discusses that synod, says the reverse: Benevento describes the distribution of ashes as already generally observed by clerics and laity, and the homilist Ælfric assumed the practice for all classes of people nearly a hundred years earlier. Benevento records the custom; it did not create it.

    The Latin name is old too: dies cinerum, "day of ashes," appears in the earliest surviving copies of the Gregorian Sacramentary and probably dates to at least the eighth century. Why the season starts on a Wednesday is less settled than most accounts admit. The Wednesday exists to yield exactly forty fast days once the six Sundays are excluded, but sources place the change anywhere from the late fifth to the seventh century. In Gregory the Great's Rome the fast ran six weeks of six days — thirty-six days, not forty.

    Timeline

    • c. 200 — Tertullian attests penitents at Rome in sackcloth, sprinkled with ashes and excluded from Communion through Lent.
    • c. 8th century — The name dies cinerum, "day of ashes," appears in the earliest surviving copies of the Gregorian Sacramentary.
    • c. 990s — The homilist Ælfric already writes of ashes as applying to all classes of people, a century before the Synod of Benevento.
    • 1091 — The Synod of Benevento describes the distribution of ashes to clerics and laity as already general practice, making it a witness to the custom rather than its origin.
    • 1549 — The first English Prayer Book replaces the blessing of ashes with the Commination service; the American Prayer Book never adopts it, and only Psalm 51 survives.
    • 1992 — The United Methodist Book of Worship adopts the first official Methodist rite using ashes, which remain optional.

    How to observe

    For Catholics between 18 and 59 this is one of only two obligatory fast days in the year, the other being Good Friday: one full meal, plus two smaller meals that together do not add up to a full one. Abstinence from meat binds from age 14. Nobody is obliged to attend Mass.

    Listen for which formula is said over you, because there are two and they are not the same prayer. "Remember that you are dust, and to dust you shall return" is the older one, and until 1970 it was the only one in the Roman Rite; "Repent and believe in the Gospel" was added beside it. Episcopalians use only the first.

    If you cannot get to a church, look for Ashes to Go, which started on a St. Louis sidewalk in 2007 and had reached at least 49 Episcopal parishes in twelve states by 2012. And if you keep your palms from Palm Sunday, you are holding next year's ashes.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Ash Wednesday a holy day of obligation?

    No. The U.S. bishops say so in as many words — "not even a Holy Day of Obligation" — which is what makes the crowds remarkable. It still ranks in the top tier of the Roman Rite's liturgical days, just after the Easter Triduum, so nothing else can displace it.

    Who has to fast, and what does fasting mean?

    Latin Catholics from age 18 to 59: one full meal plus two smaller ones that together do not equal a full meal. Abstinence from meat binds from age 14. Eastern Catholics follow their own church's law.

    Where do the ashes come from?

    From the previous year's Palm Sunday palms, burned. Catholic, Episcopal and Lutheran practice agree on this, which ties the first day of Lent to Holy Week at the other end.

    Why is the date different every year?

    It is tied to Easter — 46 days before it, or forty fast days once the six Sundays come out — so it can land anywhere from February 4 to March 11.

    Do Orthodox Christians observe Ash Wednesday?

    No. They begin Great Lent on Clean Monday, a public holiday in Greece. Their fast historically excluded Saturdays as well as Sundays, so their Lent runs eight weeks rather than six.
