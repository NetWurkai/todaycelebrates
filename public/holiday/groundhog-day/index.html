    Groundhog Day falls on February 2, when a groundhog is lifted from a stump on a hill outside Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania and a man in a top hat announces whether winter has six more weeks to run. The custom arrived with German-speaking immigrants to Pennsylvania, who had attached the same Candlemas weather lore to a badger. It is also the rare American tradition whose central claim a federal agency has tested and rejected: NOAA's published track record finds no predictive skill in it.

    History

    February 2 is Candlemas, and the day carried weather lore in Europe for centuries before any groundhog was involved. John Ray's 1678 collection preserves the couplet in its most familiar form: "If Candlemas day be fair and bright / Winter will have another flight." In German-speaking Europe the day was also Dachstag, Badger Day, and the animal watched was the badger — if it saw its shadow it went back underground for four more weeks. That is the Library of Congress's account, following the folklorist Don Yoder. The Punxsutawney Groundhog Club's own history says hedgehog instead, and the two versions have never been reconciled.

    Pennsylvania Dutch immigration ran from about 1727 to 1775, and the Library of Congress judges it likely that the American custom took shape in that period — likely, not documented. No source traces an unbroken line from European practice to the American one. The earliest known American reference, a Pennsylvania diary entry of 1840 found by Yoder, has the groundhog returning underground for forty days, not six weeks.

    Punxsutawney enters the record in 1886, when the club says the phrase "groundhog day" first appeared in the local newspaper. February 2 of the following year, 1887, brought the first organized trek to Gobbler's Knob. Who organized it is contested: the Hagley Museum and Library credits the Punxsutawney Groundhog Club under the town's newspaper editor, Clymer H. Freas; the Library of Congress credits the local Elks Lodge. The groundhog was not called Punxsutawney Phil until the 1960s.

    Timeline

    • 1678 — John Ray prints the Candlemas couplet in its familiar form: "If Candlemas day be fair and bright / Winter will have another flight."
    • 1727–1775 — The period of Pennsylvania Dutch immigration in which the Library of Congress says the American holiday likely took shape.
    • 1840 — The earliest known American reference to groundhog weather prediction, a Pennsylvania diary entry, which gives forty days rather than six weeks.
    • 1886 — The phrase "groundhog day" first appears in the Punxsutawney newspaper, according to the Punxsutawney Groundhog Club.
    • February 2, 1887 — The first organized trek to Gobbler's Knob. Hagley credits the Groundhog Club; the Library of Congress credits the local Elks Lodge.
    • December 27, 2006 — The 1993 film Groundhog Day is added to the Library of Congress's National Film Registry.

    How to observe

    Gobbler's Knob sits almost two miles uphill from the Punxsutawney parking lots, and the club runs buses from three designated areas starting at 3 a.m. The stage entertainment starts then too, fireworks go off just after 6:30, and the prognostication happens at sunrise, around 7:20. It is standing room only in a rustic field in February: the club advises cash, no backpacks or bags, and says small children should not attend. Pennsylvania's tourism office streamed the 2026 ceremony free at visitpa.com with a second feed at pacast.com, and its Groundhog Day page is where the following year's links appear.

    The better reason to mark the day is NOAA's scorecard. "Grading the groundhogs" ranks nineteen prognosticating animals with records of twenty years or more, and Punxsutawney Phil places seventeenth. Staten Island Chuck leads at 85 percent. In the 2026 edition, second place went to Lander Lil, who is a statue of a prairie dog in Wyoming. Reading that list teaches more about forecasting than any explanation of why a groundhog cannot do it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Groundhog Day always February 2?

    Yes. The date is fixed. In 2027 it falls on a Tuesday.

    How accurate is Punxsutawney Phil?

    Four federal figures exist and they do not agree. NOAA's "Grading the groundhogs" puts him at 35 percent, scored against U.S. February and March temperature averages from 2006 to 2025. NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information says 30 percent over the past ten years. The Library of Congress says about 40 percent and cites nothing for it. The most useful number is the raw one: NOAA's published track record for 1887 through 2017 counts 103 shadows, 18 no-shadows and 10 years with no record, and concludes that the table shows "no predictive skill for the groundhog during the most recent years of this analysis."

    Was the original animal a badger or a hedgehog?

    Contested. The Library of Congress, following Don Yoder, and Smithsonian Magazine both say badger — Candlemas was Dachstag, Badger Day, across much of German-speaking Europe. The Punxsutawney Groundhog Club says German lore used the hedgehog. Nothing resolves it.

    Where does "six more weeks of winter" come from?

    Not from the European lore, as far as the record goes. The German badger version gave four more weeks. The 1840 Pennsylvania diary and Hagley's account of the Candlemas belief both give forty days. No reputable source traces the six-week figure back to Europe.

    Is Groundhog Day a federal holiday?

    No. Federal offices, banks and post offices operate normally. Pennsylvania promotes the day through its state tourism office, which is a different thing.
