    Use Your Common Sense Day is listed on November 4 by daily-holiday calendars that disagree with each other about who created it and cannot show where the date came from. November 4 does carry a real observance, and a statutory one: Oklahoma has marked Will Rogers Day on that date since 1947, because it is the humorist's birthday. The link between Rogers and the common-sense day, on the other hand, exists only on the calendar sites.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. Holiday aggregators carry it and do not agree: two credit a career coach named Bud Bilanich, one of those giving 2015 as a start date, and a third names no founder and reaches instead for Will Rogers. No museum, archive, government body or news outlet documents a founder, year or sponsor, and nothing in the record connects Rogers to the observance — that link is a coincidence of dates, asserted on calendar sites and nowhere else.

    The Will Rogers part of November 4 is real, and it is in statute. Title 25, section 86 of the Oklahoma Statutes, enacted in 1947, reads: "The fourth day of November each year is hereby declared a public holiday, to be known as 'Will Rogers Day'." It is commemorative rather than closing — the day is absent from the list of holidays on which Oklahoma state employees get a paid day off, and the statute specifies that business proceeds as on any other day. Claremore has celebrated the birthday since the Will Rogers Memorial opened there on November 4, 1938, with Franklin Roosevelt paying tribute over the radio.

    And the quotation — "common sense ain't common," attributed to Rogers on practically every page that marks this day? There is no evidence he said it. The phrase appears nowhere in his complete published weekly articles and daily telegrams, about 1.48 million words, and nowhere in the Library of Congress's digitized newspapers. The museum that holds his writings warns at the top of its own quotations page that "Some quotes attributed to Will Rogers are falsely credited." The sentiment predates him anyway — a version appears in the New-York Tribune in 1878, a year before he was born. What he did write, of Calvin Coolidge campaigning on common sense in 1924, is sharper: "Common Sense is not an issue in politics, it's an affliction."

    Timeline

    • November 4, 1879 — William Penn Adair Rogers is born in Indian Territory near Oologah, in the Cherokee Nation's Cooweescoowee District.
    • 1915 — Rogers signs with Florenz Ziegfeld; by 1918 he is a Broadway star in the Ziegfeld Follies.
    • December 24, 1922 — His first weekly column appears in the New York Times; the McNaught Syndicate takes it national a week later. He adds a daily telegram in 1926.
    • August 15, 1935 — Rogers and the aviator Wiley Post are killed when their plane crashes near Barrow, Alaska.
    • November 4, 1938 — The Will Rogers Memorial is dedicated in Claremore, Oklahoma, on what would have been his fifty-ninth birthday.
    • 1947 — Oklahoma declares November 4 a public holiday by statute, to be known as Will Rogers Day.

    How to observe

    Read what he actually wrote. The Will Rogers Memorial Museum hosts free PDFs of the complete Oklahoma State University Press editions of his Weekly Articles and Daily Telegrams, edited from his surviving typescripts; ten minutes in the 1920s volumes is a better use of the day than any quotation graphic. To see the columns as readers did, the Library of Congress's Chronicling America carries them in small-town papers under a Beverly Hills dateline.

    One correction worth having before planning a trip: the Will Rogers ranch house in Pacific Palisades, California burned in the Palisades Fire of January 2025 — the thirty-one-room house and the stables destroyed, though staff evacuated the horses and more than 150 artifacts, his typewriter among them. The park reopened with limited access in November 2025: grounds and polo field, no house. The museum in Claremore and the Birthplace Ranch in Oologah, both run by the Oklahoma Historical Society, are open.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who started Use Your Common Sense Day?

    Nobody who can be verified. No founder, founding year or sponsor appears in any museum, archive, government or news source, and the holiday calendars that carry the day do not agree among themselves.

    Is November 4 Will Rogers's birthday?

    Yes — November 4, 1879. The Oklahoma Historical Society, the Will Rogers Memorial Museum and the Smithsonian's National Portrait Gallery all give the same date. But his birthday is why Oklahoma marks November 4; it is not a documented reason for this observance.

    Is it an official holiday anywhere?

    Not as Use Your Common Sense Day. November 4 is Will Rogers Day in Oklahoma, a public holiday in state statute since 1947. It is commemorative: state offices do not close.

    Did Will Rogers say "common sense ain't common"?

    There is no evidence for it. The phrase is absent from his complete published columns and from the Library of Congress's digitized newspapers, and the museum that holds his papers warns that quotations attributed to him are often falsely credited.

    What is Will Rogers actually known for?

    Rope tricks in vaudeville, Ziegfeld Follies stardom from 1915, a syndicated column from December 1922 that ran in hundreds of papers, a top-fifteen Sunday radio program, and more than seventy films.

    Notes on sourcing. The aggregator claims about this observance's founder are described in the text but cited nowhere as evidence; nationaltoday, daysoftheyear and timeanddate were read only to characterize what circulates. On the number of papers carrying his column, the Oklahoma Historical Society says more than five hundred and the scholarly editors say approximately six hundred; the page says "hundreds" rather than averaging. On the column count, the Memorial Museum's "over 3,600" is followed over the National Portrait Gallery's "four thousand," because the museum holds the writings. The quotation finding is a negative, and it is stated as such: he also spoke on stage, on radio and in interviews, which the published columns do not capture, so the claim here is that the line cannot be sourced to his published writing or to any digitized newspaper of record — not that he provably never said it. No Oklahoma gubernatorial proclamation is claimed, because the statute is self-executing and none was found. Rejected as sources: nationaltoday, nationaldaycalendar, checkiday, timeanddate, daysoftheyear, quote-aggregator sites and content farms.
