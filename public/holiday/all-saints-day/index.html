    All Saints' Day falls on November 1 in the Western churches and honors all the saints of the church, both the named and the unknown, who have attained heaven. The Eastern churches keep the same commemoration on the first Sunday after Pentecost instead. Its origin cannot be traced with certainty, and the November 1 date arrived long after the feast itself — by way of a Roman chapel dedication, an English monk's calendar, and finally a papal order.

    History

    The feast is older than its November date. According to Ephraem Syrus, who died around 373, a feast of all martyrs was kept on May 13 in the Eastern church, and that may be why Pope Boniface IV chose May 13 when he dedicated the Pantheon in Rome as a church in honor of the Blessed Virgin and all martyrs in 609. Its origin cannot be traced with certainty, and no single pope founded it.

    The first evidence for November 1, and for widening the feast from martyrs to all saints, comes from the reign of Pope Gregory III, who held the office from 731 to 741 and dedicated a chapel in St. Peter's in Rome on November 1 in honor of all saints. Alcuin kept the day on November 1 in 800, and it appears on that date in a ninth-century English calendar. In 837 Pope Gregory IV ordered its general observance — a date popular retellings usually give as 835.

    In medieval England the festival was known as All Hallows, which is where its eve gets the name Halloween.

    The obligation attached to the day is current law, not history. Canon 1246 of the Code of Canon Law names All Saints among the days that must be observed in the universal Church alongside Sunday, and lets a conference of bishops suppress some of them or move them to a Sunday with the Apostolic See's prior approval. The United States bishops kept this one: their decree of December 13, 1991 retains "November 1, the solemnity of All Saints," confirmed on July 4, 1992 and effective January 1, 1993.

    Timeline

    • Before about 373 — A feast of all martyrs is kept on May 13 in the Eastern church, according to Ephraem Syrus, who dies around this year.
    • 609 — Pope Boniface IV dedicates the Pantheon in Rome as a church in honor of the Blessed Virgin and all martyrs, on May 13.
    • 731 to 741 — Pope Gregory III dedicates a chapel in St. Peter's on November 1 in honor of all saints: the first evidence for the November 1 date and for broadening the feast to all saints.
    • 800 — Alcuin keeps All Saints' Day on November 1; the date also appears in a ninth-century English calendar.
    • 837 — Pope Gregory IV orders the feast's general observance.
    • December 13, 1991 — The United States bishops retain November 1 as a holy day of obligation; the Apostolic See confirms it July 4, 1992, effective January 1, 1993.

    How to observe

    Check whether the Mass obligation actually applies this year before assuming it does. The same United States norm that retains the day carves out an exception: whenever November 1 falls on a Saturday or on a Monday, the precept to attend Mass is abrogated. The clause covers only January 1, August 15, and November 1, so the answer changes from year to year. In 2026 it falls on a Sunday, which the clause does not cover.

    Read the primary documents rather than a summary. Canon 1246 is two sentences long and posted in full on the Vatican's site; the United States norm that modifies it is a single page on the bishops' conference site.

    If you keep the day in an Eastern church, the date is the first Sunday after Pentecost — worth knowing before making plans with someone on the other calendar.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is All Saints' Day always November 1?

    In the Western churches, yes. The Eastern churches keep the commemoration on the first Sunday after Pentecost, so it moves with Easter.

    Is All Saints' Day a federal holiday in the United States?

    No. The federal holidays are those established by 5 U.S.C. 6103 and listed by the Office of Personnel Management, and no November 1 observance appears among them. Offices, banks, and post offices operate normally.

    Is All Saints' Day a holy day of obligation?

    In the Latin Rite dioceses of the United States, yes — one of six days the bishops retained in their 1991 decree. But the same norm abrogates the precept to attend Mass whenever November 1 falls on a Saturday or a Monday, so in some years it does not apply.

    Who started All Saints' Day?

    No one person. The origin cannot be traced with certainty: Gregory III is the first evidence for the November 1 date, and Gregory IV ordered general observance in 837, but the feast existed earlier on May 13.

    What is the difference between All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day?

    All Saints' Day, November 1, honors the saints in heaven. All Souls' Day, November 2, remembers the dead more broadly. With Halloween they form a stretch sometimes called Allhallowtide.
