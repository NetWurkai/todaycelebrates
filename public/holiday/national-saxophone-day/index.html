    National Saxophone Day falls on November 6, the birthday of Antoine-Joseph "Adolphe" Sax, born in Dinant, in what is now Belgium's Namur province, on November 6, 1814. The observance's own origin is not documented, but the date is not arbitrary: it is the inventor's, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art opened its Sax bicentennial display on November 6, 2014. Sax patented an instrument that conservatories resisted for most of a century and jazz made unavoidable.

    History

    Sax was born into the trade. His father, Charles-Joseph Sax, was an instrument maker in Dinant, and Adolphe was filing patents on clarinet improvements from 1835. The saxophone grew out of attempts between roughly 1838 and 1840 to improve the bass clarinet. He was guarded about it: when the new instrument was shown to the jury of the Belgian industrial exposition in 1841, it was played from behind a curtain.

    Belgium was too small for the ambition. Sax settled permanently in Paris in 1842 and opened an instrument factory there in July 1843, and it was in Paris that he worked out the full family of seven, from sopranino to contrabass. He deposited the French patent for a system of wind instruments called saxophones on March 21, 1846. The design laid the family out twice over, in two groups of seven: one pitched for the orchestra, one for bands and the military. That second group tells you where he expected the money to be.

    He was right about the bands. In 1845 French military bands tested competing instruments on the Champ de Mars; Sax's stood out, and the reorganization of regimental music that followed adopted his saxhorns, saxophones and saxotrombas. In 1857 he was appointed at the Paris Conservatoire, teaching saxophone to military students. Concert-hall acceptance took far longer: the Conservatoire did not open a saxophone class of its own until 1942.

    Commercial success never followed. Rival makers sued him over his patents repeatedly, and he went bankrupt three times fighting them, weakened further by the revolution of 1848 and the war of 1870. When his company failed in 1877, his private collection of 467 instruments was sold off and dispersed; 33 of those pieces are now at the Musical Instruments Museum in Brussels. Sax died in Paris on February 7, 1894, and was buried at Montmartre Cemetery. The instrument's real public arrived afterward, in New Orleans between about 1920 and 1925.

    How to observe

    Go to Dinant if you are anywhere near it. La Maison de Monsieur Sax stands at Rue Sax 37, on the spot where he was born, is free, and opens daily from 9:00 to 19:00. It calls itself an interpretive center rather than a museum, and it does not skip the bankruptcies.

    Otherwise, listen chronologically instead of by favorite. Start with the military band repertoire the instrument was actually patented for, then the New Orleans recordings of the early 1920s, then a player from the bebop era. You can hear the instrument's purpose being rewritten underneath it, which is the thing worth noticing on Sax's birthday.

    If you want something concrete: Sax's funerary chapel at Montmartre had fallen into disrepair, and the Association Internationale Adolphe Sax in Dinant has been raising money to restore it, with work due to begin in spring 2027.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why is National Saxophone Day on November 6?

    It is Adolphe Sax's birthday: he was born in Dinant on November 6, 1814. Who first proposed the observance, and when, is not recorded in any source that can be checked.

    When was the saxophone patented?

    The French patent was deposited on March 21, 1846. That is the date given by Wallonia's official biographical dictionary, which quotes the patent itself, and it is matched by the Philharmonie de Paris and the Belgian public broadcaster RTBF. The Library of Congress's music blog instead marks the saxophone patent anniversary in late June; this page follows the sources that name the French patent and its date directly.

    Was Adolphe Sax Belgian or French?

    Both, in the way that matters. He was born in Dinant, in present-day Belgium, and did all of the saxophone work in Paris, where he lived from 1842 until his death in 1894.

    Did Sax get rich from the saxophone?

    No. He went bankrupt three times defending his patents against rival makers, and his collection of 467 instruments was sold off when his company failed in 1877.

    How many saxophones are in the family?

    Seven in the original design — sopranino, soprano, alto, tenor, baritone, bass and contrabass — and the patent laid them out twice, once for the orchestra and once for military bands.
