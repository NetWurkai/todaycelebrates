    National Chinese Take-Out Day falls on November 5 and has no documented founder — but the object it celebrates has one of the best-documented origins in American food. The folded paper carton is a patented invention: US Patent 529,053, granted in 1894 to a Chicago man, for a pail to carry oysters. Chinese food arrived in it later, and the story of how runs through American immigration law as much as through cooking.

    History

    The container came first, and it had nothing to do with Chinese food. On November 13, 1894, the Patent Office granted patent 529,053, "Paper Pail," to Frederick Weeks Wilcox of Chicago — a pail folded from a single piece of heavy paper, whose fifth claim is for "an oyster pail formed from a single piece of foldable material." Folded oyster pails already existed; what Wilcox patented was a leak-resistant construction, and the document names no food but oysters. The Bloomer Brothers of New York began making the containers in 1900; that firm became Fold-Pak, which the Associated Press reports still makes most of the takeout cartons used in the United States.

    Chinese restaurants in America are older than the carton. Smithsonian Magazine dates the first to 1849, when Norman Asing opened Macao and Woosung at Kearny and Commercial streets in San Francisco; the Chinese Historical Society of America credits the Canton Restaurant to the same Gold Rush year. The Library of Congress records that 20,000 of the 67,000 people who landed in California in 1852 came from China.

    What turned restaurants into an industry was immigration law. The Chinese Exclusion Act, approved May 6, 1882, suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers but exempted merchants. Historian Heather Lee's account for the American Social History Project is that officials at first treated restaurant owners as laborers who could not re-enter the country, and that in 1915 they relented, conceding that Chinese restaurants were significant enterprises and granting their proprietors merchant status. Lee found the number of Chinese eateries in New York City quadrupled between 1910 and 1920; Chinese restaurants had become the largest employer of Chinese immigrants by mid-century. The exclusion acts were repealed in 1943.

    Delivery is older than most people assume. The earliest restaurant delivery advertisement TIME could locate is a 1922 notice for the Kin-Chu café in Los Angeles, which called itself the only place on the West Coast making and delivering real Chinese food, hot, as late as one in the morning. The pagoda printed on the carton is much newer: the Associated Press dates that design to the 1970s.

    Timeline

    • 1849 — Chinese restaurants open in San Francisco during the Gold Rush. Smithsonian Magazine credits Norman Asing's Macao and Woosung; the Chinese Historical Society of America credits the Canton Restaurant.
    • May 6, 1882 — The Chinese Exclusion Act is approved, suspending the immigration of Chinese laborers while exempting merchants.
    • November 13, 1894 — US Patent 529,053 is granted to Frederick Weeks Wilcox of Chicago for a folded "Paper Pail" — in the patent's own words, an oyster pail.
    • 1915 — Immigration officials concede that Chinese restaurants are significant enterprises and extend merchant status to their proprietors.
    • 1922 — The Kin-Chu café in Los Angeles advertises hot Chinese food delivered until 1 a.m., the earliest restaurant delivery advertisement TIME could locate.
    • 1970s — Fold-Pak adds the pagoda design to the carton.

    How to observe

    Order from a single-location restaurant and read past the standard American-Chinese set on the menu. Regional menus are common and often go unadvertised in English.

    Then look at the patent. US 529,053 is online, short and illustrated, and seeing the takeout box diagrammed as a vessel for shellfish is a small, strange pleasure — the shape every American recognizes, drawn in 1894 for a different dinner entirely.

    The National Archives' guide to Chinese immigration records describes the exclusion-era case files: merchants' testimony and passports, partnership lists, photographs. That paperwork is the trail behind a great many American Chinese restaurants, and it is open to researchers.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who started National Chinese Take-Out Day?

    No reliable source names a founder, an organization or a founding year. It appears to be a creation of holiday listings rather than of anyone in the restaurant business.

    Why is the Chinese takeout box shaped like that?

    Because it was designed to carry oysters. US Patent 529,053, granted in 1894 to Frederick Weeks Wilcox of Chicago, covers a leak-resistant pail folded from one piece of paper, and the patent's own claim language calls it an oyster pail.

    Is chop suey American or Chinese?

    Contested, and worth saying so. Smithsonian Magazine reports that few people agree on the dish's provenance, and that the story crediting the Chinese statesman Li Hongzhang with inventing it on his 1896 visit to the United States is a myth, since the dish was already eaten there. Writing in History Today, Alexander Lee argues it is Cantonese in origin, probably from Taishan in Guangdong. What is clearly American is the scale: the first chop suey recipe in Good Housekeeping ran in 1895.

    How many Chinese restaurants are there in the United States?

    No agency publishes a count. Pew Research Center, using point-of-interest data for March 2023, found 787,153 open US restaurants, that 12 percent serve Asian food, and that 39 percent of Asian restaurants are Chinese — roughly 37,000 by arithmetic, though Pew publishes no such figure.

    Did Chinese restaurants invent food delivery?

    Not exactly, but they were early. TIME describes Chinese restaurants as well ahead of the American delivery trend, and the oldest delivery advertisement it located is the Kin-Chu café's, from 1922.
