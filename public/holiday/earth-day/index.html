    Earth Day falls on April 22 and began as one day of American protest in 1970, proposed by Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin and organized in about six months. The U.S. Senate's own history puts the turnout at roughly 20 million people, across some 2,000 college campuses and 10,000 schools, though no source names who counted. It has been international since 1990, and it has never been a federal holiday.

    History

    Nelson, a Democrat, announced the idea at a conference in Seattle on September 20, 1969, having seen the Santa Barbara oil spill earlier that year: a blowout at Union Oil's well 21 under Platform A, five and a half miles offshore, which released about 100,000 barrels before it was stopped in December. He asked his staff to work out how to sponsor nationwide campus environmental teach-ins the following spring. They picked the week of April 19 to 25 and settled on Wednesday, April 22, a weekday after winter weather and before final exams, to get the largest possible student turnout. Nelson recruited a Republican congressman, Paul "Pete" McCloskey, as co-chair so the day carried no partisan lean, and hired Denis Hayes, then a graduate student at Harvard, to organize it.

    So many members of Congress were out at Earth Day events that Congress went into recess. Rallies in Chicago, New York and Philadelphia drew as many as 25,000 each, about 10,000 gathered at the Washington Monument, and Fifth Avenue was closed to traffic.

    What the day caused is harder to establish than what followed it. The EPA's own archive is willing to say that the public pressure Earth Day created "provided the necessary impetus" for the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments, and that the timing was no accident. The agency itself came later that year: the Senate confirmed its first administrator on December 2, 1970, the date EPA treats as its birthday. The Clean Water Act of 1972 and the Endangered Species Act of 1973 are same-era legislation, and the government sources documenting them do not credit Earth Day.

    Timeline

    • January 28, 1969 — A blowout at Union Oil's well 21 off Santa Barbara, California releases about 100,000 barrels of oil before it is stopped in December.
    • September 20, 1969 — Senator Gaylord Nelson announces plans for a nationwide environmental demonstration at a conference in Seattle.
    • April 22, 1970 — The first Earth Day, a Wednesday, held as a nationwide environmental teach-in. Congress goes into recess.
    • December 2, 1970 — The Senate confirms the Environmental Protection Agency's first administrator, the date EPA marks as its birthday.
    • 1990 — Earth Day goes global, again organized by Denis Hayes.
    • April 22, 2016 — The Paris Agreement opens for signature at UN headquarters in New York; 175 Parties sign, 174 countries and the European Union.

    How to observe

    Register a cleanup. EARTHDAY.ORG runs The Great Global Cleanup, accepts registrations year-round, puts each event on a public map, and publishes guides for individuals and for groups.

    If you would rather contribute data than carry bags, the EPA maintains a list of participatory science projects open to the public. Among them is the Cyanobacteria Monitoring Collaborative, whose volunteers photograph and identify algal blooms through the bloomWatch app, and the Chesapeake Monitoring Cooperative's water quality work. The National Park Service runs its own citizen science and BioBlitz programs.

    The archive option is better than it sounds. The Wisconsin Historical Society holds the Gaylord Nelson papers: 1,040 boxes of paper, audio recordings, film, photographs and scrapbooks, open to the public during archive hours, with a finding aid online. The Society has also published an Earth Day gallery of 130 photographs spanning 1954 to 2006.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Earth Day?

    April 22, every year. In 2027 it falls on a Thursday.

    Is Earth Day a federal holiday?

    No. The Congressional Research Service records resolutions in the 92nd, 93rd and 106th Congresses to designate an annual Earth Day, on none of which further action was taken.

    Did 20 million Americans really take part in 1970?

    The figure is everywhere and traceable to no count. EARTHDAY.ORG, the U.S. Senate's historical office, the EPA and Encyclopaedia Britannica all give about 20 million. The Wisconsin Historical Society's own archivist puts it at 10 to 20 million. The Congressional Research Service declines a number altogether and cites the New York Times of April 23, 1970, which reported "millions." The historian Adam Rome makes the more useful point: it was not one event but at least 12,000 of them.

    Did Earth Day create the EPA?

    Not directly, on the government's own account. EPA was established by Reorganization Plan No. 3, which President Nixon sent to Congress on July 9, 1970; the Senate confirmed the first administrator on December 2. EARTHDAY.ORG says the first Earth Day led to the agency's creation, while EPA's own history pages set out the sequence without making that claim. Where EPA does assert a causal link is the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments.

    Why April 22?

    For the academic calendar. Nelson's staff wanted a midweek day after winter weather had passed but before final exams, to maximize student participation, and April 22, 1970 was a Wednesday.

    Source conflict, resolved and disclosed: the 1970 participation figure. Five sources give about 20 million, the Wisconsin Historical Society's own archivist gives a range of 10 to 20 million, and the Congressional Research Service declines a figure and falls back on the contemporaneous New York Times, which said only "millions." This page reports the spread and names who says what rather than averaging, because no source traces 20 million to a count. On causation the page follows the more primary source in each case: EPA's archive where EPA itself claims Earth Day as an impetus (the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments), and the documenting agency where it makes no such claim (the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act), rather than EARTHDAY.ORG's broader account.

    Not claimed, for want of a source: that April 22 was chosen because it is Vladimir Lenin's birthday — no reputable source supports it, and the one acceptable source that touches the question renders the dates in a way that appears to be mistaken, so this page does not rely on it and gives only the documented academic-calendar reason; the enactment dates of the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments and the 1972 Clean Water Act, which EPA's own history pages do not state; any methodology or primary count behind the 20 million figure; Denis Hayes's exact age on the day itself (Harvard dates him at 25 in 1969); Pete McCloskey's district or the formal title of his role; the country counts for 1990 (EARTHDAY.ORG says 141, Britannica says more than 140) and the participation figures for 2000, 2010 and the present day, which are self-reported by the organizing body and uncorroborated; the sales figures usually given for Rachel Carson's Silent Spring; and any government assessment linking the Santa Barbara spill to the environmental movement or to legislation — NOAA confines itself to the incident and describes its environmental effects as relatively minimal.

    Rejected as sources: nationaltoday, nationaldaycalendar, checkiday, timeanddate, history.com, and content-farm or AI-generated listicles. Advocacy and opinion outlets were also set aside on the Lenin question, which is why that claim is left unaddressed beyond the note above.
