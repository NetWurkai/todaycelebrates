    National Cappuccino Day falls on November 8 and has no traceable founder, which is ordinary for a drink-themed observance and worth saying out loud. The drink itself has a much better paper trail: a Viennese ancestor sweetened with whipped cream, an Italian reinvention that had to wait on a machine, and an American arrival that took fifty years to become ordinary.

    History

    The observance has no documented origin. No founding organization, proclamation, or first observance could be sourced for National Cappuccino Day, and the aggregator pages that promote it do not agree with one another — at least one places it in October. That is the whole honest account of the day.

    The drink is better served by the record. Encyclopaedia Britannica traces the first attestation of cappuccino to the coffeehouses of eighteenth-century Vienna, where the Kapuziner was coffee with whipped cream, usually sweetened. The name is a color comparison: the brown hooded robe of a Capuchin friar worn over a white tonsured head. The same resemblance gave the capuchin monkey its name. Merriam-Webster dates the English word to 1893, decades before the drink an American orders today existed, which suggests that the early English use described the Viennese ancestor rather than the modern cup.

    The Italian cappuccino is a twentieth-century drink, and it waited on hardware. Britannica places its appearance in the early twentieth century, after the invention of the espresso machine. It names no inventor and no patent year, and no acceptable source reached for this page supplies one either. The historian Jonathan Morris, writing in Made in Italy: Rethinking a Century of Italian Design, identifies 1948 to 1962 as the critical years for the emergence of the modern espresso machine. Britannica supplies the commercial fact underneath that: the early machines were expensive and cumbersome, and became less costly and more widely available after World War II. Only then did cappuccino become the Italian breakfast drink of choice.

    It is a specific construction rather than coffee with milk in it. Britannica describes equal parts espresso, steamed milk, and frothed milk in three distinct layers, with the foam spooned on in dollops, against a latte's one part espresso to two parts steamed milk under a thin layer of foam.

    Timeline

    • 18th century — Viennese coffeehouses serve the Kapuziner, coffee with whipped cream and usually sugar: the first attestation of the drink.
    • 1893 — the earliest known use of the English word "cappuccino."
    • Early 20th century — the Italian cappuccino appears, after the invention of the espresso machine.
    • 1930s — cappuccino is served in Italian restaurants in the United States, as early as this decade.
    • 1948-1962 — the critical years for the emergence of the modern Italian espresso machine.
    • 1980s — cappuccino becomes widespread in the United States with the rise of nationwide coffee chains.

    How to observe

    Order one before eleven in the morning, and notice that the rule is real. Britannica records that Italians traditionally avoid milk after breakfast, and that cappuccinos are seldom served after 10:00 or 11:00 a.m.; a plain espresso is the afternoon order. The custom is worth keeping for a day even at home, if only to settle whether a cappuccino genuinely tastes better at seven in the morning.

    Build the foam the way the definition says. Spoon it on in dollops instead of pouring it, and what you get is the layered drink Britannica is describing rather than the poured, latte-like cup most cafes now serve. Set it beside a latte and the ratio is easy to taste: equal thirds against one-to-two.

    If the American half of the story interests you, the Library of Congress's Chronicling America archive of digitized newspapers is searchable by town and by decade. Britannica says cappuccino reached Italian restaurants in the United States as early as the 1930s. The menus, advertisements, and restaurant columns of that decade are where you can watch it arrive, in a city you know, without taking anyone's word for it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who started National Cappuccino Day?

    No one who can be sourced. No founding organization, proclamation, or first observance is documented, and the November 8 date appears only on daily-holiday aggregators, which do not agree with one another.

    What is the difference between a cappuccino and a latte?

    A cappuccino is equal parts espresso, steamed milk, and frothed milk, layered, with the foam spooned on top. A latte is one part espresso to two parts steamed milk under only a thin layer of foam, which makes it creamier, sweeter, and higher in calories, fat, and protein.

    Why is it called a cappuccino?

    For the color. The word means "Capuchin" in Italian, after the brown hooded habit of the Capuchin friars worn over a white tonsured head. The same comparison named the capuchin monkey.

    Is it true that Italians will not drink one after breakfast?

    Largely, yes. The tradition holds that milk impedes digestion after a meal, and cappuccinos are seldom served after 10:00 or 11:00 in the morning. Visitors who want coffee later in the day order an espresso instead.

    When did cappuccino reach the United States?

    Italian restaurants in the United States served it as early as the 1930s, but it became widespread only in the 1980s, with the rise of nationwide coffee chains.
