    Mardi Gras

    Celebration

    Mardi Gras is Shrove Tuesday, the day immediately before Ash Wednesday, and the last day of the Carnival season. In Louisiana it is not a figure of speech but a statutory one: state law lists "the day of Mardi Gras" among the state's days of public rest and legal holidays. Almost everything Americans picture when they hear the word — krewes, floats, purple and green and gold — was worked out in New Orleans between the 1850s and the 1880s.

    History

    The name is French for Fat Tuesday, which Britannica traces to "the custom of using all the fats in the home before Lent." Shrove Tuesday is "the day immediately preceding Ash Wednesday (the beginning of Lent in Western Christian churches)." Pinned to Ash Wednesday rather than to a calendar date, the day moves every year; the New Orleans Public Library's City Archives publish the dates from 1947 through 2050.

    Louisiana dates its own connection to 1699. "Since 1699, when Pierre Le Moyne, Sieur d'Iberville celebrated his arrival at the mouth of the Mississippi on Shrove Tuesday, Mardi Gras has been integrally linked to Louisiana's cultural heritage," the Louisiana State Museum writes. Evidence for the century that follows is thin — the museum notes "scant evidence of Carnival during the early 1700s" — but by 1781 the season was established enough that Spanish colonial authorities forbade free people of color and enslaved people to mask or mimic whites during it. The first parades were improvised, "impromptu processions formed by maskers already in the streets," with young Creole men organizing informal processions by the 1830s.

    The modern machinery arrives in the 1850s. The City Archives date the Mistick Krewe of Comus to 1856 and record that "the first Mardi Gras parade was held in 1857, featuring the Mistick Krewe of Comus"; the state museum instead describes the krewe as emerging in 1857, and both years are reported below rather than reconciled. Comus staged a nighttime procession followed by a private ball, and set the pattern for everything after it, secrecy included: "the aura of mystery and surprise associated with krewes to this day was conceived by Comus."

    Others followed quickly: the Twelfth Night Revelers in 1870, the Knights of Momus and Rex in 1872, the Krewe of Proteus in 1882. Rex supplied the parts that now read as official, billing its monarchs as the king and queen of Carnival and introducing a daytime parade. Purple, green and gold, the City Archives state, "are the official colors of carnival and represent justice, faith, and power."

    The sharpest modern break came in 1992, when the city tied parade permits to Councilmember Dorothy Mae Taylor's ordinance barring discrimination in krewe membership. Comus, Momus and Proteus all stopped parading that year; Proteus resumed in 2000, and the Comus and Momus parades never returned.

    Timeline

    • 1699 — Pierre Le Moyne, Sieur d'Iberville celebrates his arrival at the mouth of the Mississippi on Shrove Tuesday.
    • 1781 — Spanish colonial authorities forbid free people of color and enslaved people to mask or mimic whites during the Carnival season.
    • 1857 — The first Mardi Gras parade, staged by the Mistick Krewe of Comus, which the City Archives date to 1856.
    • 1872 — Rex and the Knights of Momus are founded; Rex introduces a daytime parade and the king and queen of Carnival.
    • 1896 — Les Mysterieuses becomes the first large-scale women's krewe.
    • 1992 — A city ordinance ties parade permits to non-discrimination in krewe membership; Comus, Momus and Proteus stop parading. Proteus returns in 2000.

    How to observe

    Find out whether you actually have the day off, because the answer is unusually technical. Louisiana lists "the day of Mardi Gras" among its statewide legal holidays and names it for state employees, but local closure is a separate question: the statute names twenty-nine parishes, plus all municipalities, where "Mardi Gras shall be a holiday when the governing authorities so declare by ordinance."

    Leave the French Quarter. Rex, the King of Carnival, parades Mardi Gras morning, and the mayor of New Orleans traditionally toasts the kings and queens of Rex and Zulu on the steps of Gallier Hall.

    For the older tradition rather than the spectacle, go to a rural courir, or run — "a seemingly chaotic quest to obtain ingredients for a communal gumbo" that in fact "involves a complex system of rules." Because courirs were held in isolated areas where illiteracy was prevalent, the state museum notes, "the historical record is sketchy."

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Mardi Gras?

    It has no fixed date. Mardi Gras is Shrove Tuesday, the day immediately before Ash Wednesday, so it moves each year with the start of Lent. The New Orleans Public Library's City Archives publish the dates from 1947 through 2050.

    Is Mardi Gras a public holiday?

    In Louisiana, yes: the statute listing the state's days of public rest and legal holidays includes "the day of Mardi Gras," and state departments are told that no employee shall work on it insofar as practicable. Local closures depend on local action, since the same statute lets twenty-nine named parishes and all municipalities declare it a holiday by ordinance. It is not among the 11 federal holidays in 5 U.S.C. 6103(a).

    What do purple, green and gold mean?

    They are the official colors of Carnival, and the City Archives give their meanings as justice, faith and power.

    What is a krewe?

    A private Carnival organization that stages a parade, a ball, or both. The oldest — Comus, the Twelfth Night Revelers, Momus, Rex and Proteus — are known as the old line krewes, and three of them stopped parading in 1992.
