    Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, falls on the tenth day of Tishri, nine days after Rosh Hashanah, and begins at sunset the evening before the date a civil calendar shows. It is the most widely kept fast in Judaism and the one day of the year on which the high priest of the Jerusalem Temple was permitted to enter the Holy of Holies. What the Torah actually commands is not a fast: it says to practice self-denial, and the specifics are the rabbis' work.

    History

    The day is established in Leviticus 16 and 23:26-32 and in Numbers 29:7-11. The instruction in Leviticus 23:27 is that "the tenth day of this seventh month is the Day of Atonement. It shall be a sacred occasion for you: you shall practice self-denial." The sunset-to-sunset reckoning is in the text too, at 23:32: "on the ninth day of the month at evening, from evening to evening, you shall observe this your sabbath." The Hebrew Bible also calls the day Shabbat Shabbaton, a sabbath of solemn rest. The phrase "practice self-denial" is the Jewish Publication Society's rendering; what self-denial consists of is nowhere specified.

    Leviticus 16 describes what the day looked like when there was a Temple. Aaron took two goats and cast lots over them, "one marked for GOD and the other marked for Azazel." The first was offered; the second was "left standing alive" and then, after Aaron laid both hands on its head and confessed over it "all the iniquities and transgressions of the Israelites," sent into the wilderness "through a designated agent" to "carry on it all their iniquities to an inaccessible region." Britannica, which calls that second goat the scapegoat, adds that the high priest was clothed in white linen and entered the Holy of Holies - permitted only on this day - to offer incense and sprinkle sacrificial blood.

    After the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE there was no sacrifice to make, and the day was rebuilt around what remained. Britannica records a Midrashic exchange in which Joshua ben Hananiah mourns that atonement has become impossible and his teacher Johanan ben Zakkai answers him from Hosea 6:6: "For I desire steadfast love and not sacrifice." Sacrifice gave way to repentance, judgment and good deeds, and rabbinic literature gave the day a second name, Yom ha-Din, the Day of Judgment.

    The prohibitions were set down in the Mishnah, and they arrived with an argument attached. Mishnah Yoma 8:1 forbids "eating and in drinking, and in bathing, and in smearing oil on one's body, and in wearing shoes, and in conjugal relations" - six activities, which the modern count compresses to five by treating food and drink as one. It then records Rabbi Eliezer permitting a king and a bride within thirty days of her marriage to wash their faces, and a woman after childbirth to wear shoes "because going barefoot causes her pain," followed by the flat sentence: "The Rabbis prohibit these activities."

    One element of sacrifice survived in practice rather than in law. Britannica describes kaparot, in which a chicken is waved overhead three times and then slaughtered and given to charity, and notes that the ritual "appears neither in the Torah nor the Talmud but has been in practice since at least the 9th century CE," and that it has drawn objections from rabbis and animal rights activists alike.

    Timeline

    • 70 CE - The Temple in Jerusalem is destroyed. With sacrifice no longer possible, Yom Kippur is rebuilt around repentance, judgment and good deeds, and acquires the name Yom ha-Din.
    • 2nd century CE - The Mishnah is compiled. Mishnah Yoma 8:1 sets down the prohibited activities and preserves Rabbi Eliezer's exemptions for a king, a new bride and a woman after childbirth, together with the majority's refusal of them.
    • By the 9th century CE - Kaparot is in practice, though it appears in neither the Torah nor the Talmud.
    • 11th or 12th century CE - Unetaneh Tokef - "On Rosh Hashanah it is inscribed, and on Yom Kippur it is sealed" - is attributed to Rabbi Amnon of Mainz, and may be older.
    • October 6-26, 1973 - Egypt and Syria attack Israeli forces in the Sinai Peninsula and the Golan Heights on Yom Kippur. The fighting runs to October 26 and the war takes the holiday's name.
    • October 2, 2025 - New York City's public schools close for Yom Kippur; the next closure on the published calendars is September 21, 2026.

    How to observe

    The fast runs from sunset on the ninth of Tishri until the stars come out the next evening. Britannica puts it at 25 hours; Chabad's article says "nearly 26 hours" while Chabad's own summary of the same article says 25, so take the figure as approximate and the sunset-to-nightfall span as the real rule. Britannica notes that children below the age of bar and bat mitzvah - thirteen for boys, twelve for girls - are not required to fast. Chabad directs anyone who must eat to consult both a medical professional and a rabbi.

    The day is unusual for holding five prayer services: Maariv on the eve, with Kol Nidrei; Shacharit, with a reading from Leviticus and the Yizkor memorial prayers; Musaf, which recounts the Temple service; Minchah, which includes the Book of Jonah; and Neilah, the closing of the gates, after which a single shofar blast ends the fast.

    Two things worth knowing in advance. Kol Nidre annuls vows only "insofar as they concern oneself" - obligations to other people are expressly excluded, which is the opposite of how it is usually assumed to work. And the forgiveness owed to other people is asked for before the day starts, not during it: Britannica's phrasing is that obtaining forgiveness from one's fellows signifies God's forgiveness. White is worn as a symbol of purity, sometimes as a long robe called a kittel, and gold is avoided because it evokes the golden calf.

    If you want one short primary text, read Mishnah Yoma 8:1 on Sefaria. It takes a minute, and the dissent it preserves about the king, the bride and the new mother is a better guide to how the day has been argued over than any summary of it.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Yom Kippur?

    On the tenth day of Tishri, which falls in September or October, beginning at sunset the evening before the date a civil calendar shows. In 2027 it runs from just before sunset on Sunday, October 10, to after nightfall on Monday, October 11.

    How long is the fast?

    From sunset on the ninth of Tishri until the stars appear the following evening. Britannica gives 25 hours. Chabad's article says nearly 26 while its own summary of that article says 25, so the hour figure is approximate; the sunset-to-nightfall span is the rule that matters.

    What exactly is forbidden?

    Five things, as both Britannica and Chabad present them: food and drink, bathing or washing, applying oils or creams, leather shoes, and sexual relations. Mishnah Yoma 8:1 lists six, counting eating and drinking separately.

    Who does not have to fast?

    Britannica notes that children under the age of bar and bat mitzvah - thirteen for boys, twelve for girls - are not required to fast, though older children may fast partially in preparation. Chabad tells anyone who needs to eat to consult a medical professional and a rabbi rather than deciding alone.

    Why is the 1973 war called the Yom Kippur War?

    Because Egypt and Syria attacked on the day itself, October 6, 1973, striking Israeli forces in the Sinai Peninsula and the Golan Heights. Britannica notes the date also fell during Ramadan, which is why the same war is known elsewhere as the Ramadan War or simply the October War.

    Is Yom Kippur a federal holiday in the United States?

    No. Federal law, 5 U.S.C. 6103, sets the federal holidays and Yom Kippur is not among them. Some school systems close regardless: New York City's public schools were closed on October 2, 2025, and its 2026-27 calendar closes them on September 21, 2026.
