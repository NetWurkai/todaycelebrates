    Celebration

    Chinese New Year, also called the Spring Festival, opens the year in the traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar and runs fifteen days, from the reunion dinner on New Year's Eve to the Lantern Festival. Its date moves: the Congressional Research Service puts the start at the second new moon after the winter solstice, which falls between January 21 and February 19. It is not a federal holiday in the United States, though five states have given it official recognition of some kind.

    History

    How old the festival is, the sources used for this page do not say, and this page does not guess at a date or a dynasty. What they do document is the calendar, the customs and the American half of the story.

    The calendar explains the rest. Months in the traditional calendar begin at the new moon: "Months in the Agricultural Calendar start with a new moon, which occurs when the moon and the Sun move to the same longitude on the ecliptic," says the Hong Kong Observatory, which publishes the official conversion tables. Twelve such months come to about 354 days, more than ten short of the solar year, so a leap month is inserted periodically — seven every nineteen years, on the Metonic cycle. For the new year itself the Congressional Research Service gives the rule: it "starts on the second new moon after the winter solstice." The sources do not agree on this point, and the disagreement is set out below rather than smoothed over.

    Years are named on a sixty-year cycle, not a twelve-year one: ten heavenly stems, each tied to one of five elements, pair with twelve earthly branches matching the zodiac animals. The current cycle began in 1984 and ends in 2043, and 2027, by the Observatory's table, is the Ding-wei year of the Goat.

    Most of what the season asks of people is about clearing the ground before the year turns: cleaning the house, settling debts and disputes, gathering with family, honoring elders and ancestors, and exchanging gifts of money. The cleaning is done before the new year starts, and knives and scissors are avoided because they can cut your luck. Red envelopes of lucky money, in the Smithsonian's account, come "from a custom where people swapped coins to ward off evil spirits." Dumplings are eaten partly because the shape is said to resemble parcels of money, and a whole fish is shared as a symbol of family unity. The Nian Monster, driven off by red clothing and firecrackers, is given by the Library of Congress as a legend that customs are "believed to be based on" rather than as history.

    In the United States the observance is plural and the law is patchy. The CRS states that "the Lunar New Year is not a federal holiday in the United States." California, Colorado and New Jersey have declared it an official holiday by legislation, Washington has recognized it legislatively, and New York recognizes it as a public-school holiday; some cities, San Francisco among them, close schools for the day. The Postal Service began issuing Lunar New Year stamps in 1992. The Library of Congress's photographs of the holiday in America run from Chinatown parades in Washington and San Francisco to Hmong New Year in Chico, California and Lao and Cambodian New Year in Lowell, Massachusetts.

    Timeline

    • 1984 — The current sixty-year sexagenary cycle begins; it ends in 2043.
    • 1992 — The U.S. Postal Service begins issuing special stamps to commemorate the Lunar New Year.
    • September 29, 2022 — California enacts AB 2596, declaring the Lunar New Year an official state holiday.
    • June 2, 2023 — Colorado enacts HB23-1271; New York follows on September 9, 2023 with S7573, recognizing the day as a public-school holiday.
    • January 12, 2024 — New Jersey adopts AJR201; Washington's HB 2209 follows on March 13, 2024.
    • January 28, 2025 — The Lunar New Year Day Act, H.R. 794, is introduced in the 119th Congress, alongside H.Res. 75 recognizing the year's observance.

    How to observe

    Do the work before the day, not on it. The cleaning, the debts and the disputes are meant to be finished before the new year begins, which is the part most calendars leave out.

    Treat the fifteen days as having a shape: a reunion dinner on New Year's Eve, and the Lantern Festival on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month to close it, with tangyuan. In the People's Republic the statutory holiday itself is a week long, and the travel around it, chunyun, is one of the world's largest annual migrations.

    And check the local calendar rather than assuming a single date: whether your state recognizes the day, and whether your school district closes, varies from state to state and city to city.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Chinese New Year?

    It has no fixed Gregorian date. The Congressional Research Service puts the start at the second new moon after the winter solstice, which falls between January 21 and February 19. The Hong Kong Observatory notes that calculating moon phases decades ahead carries an uncertainty of a few minutes, so a new moon close to midnight can shift a published date by a day.

    Is Chinese New Year a federal holiday in the United States?

    No. California, Colorado and New Jersey have made it an official holiday by legislation, Washington has recognized it legislatively, New York recognizes it as a public-school holiday, and some cities including San Francisco close schools for the day.

    Is it Chinese New Year or Lunar New Year?

    Both, depending on who is celebrating. In the United States the season is "often known as 'Chinese New Year,'" the Library of Congress notes, but the same new moon begins the Korean Seollal and the Vietnamese Tết.

    How long does it last?

    Fifteen days, from the reunion dinner on New Year's Eve to the Lantern Festival on the fifteenth day of the first lunar month. In the People's Republic of China the public holiday itself is one week.

    What decides the year's animal?

    Ten heavenly stems pair with twelve earthly branches matching the zodiac animals, so the combination repeats every sixty years. The Observatory's table names 2027 the Ding-wei year of the Goat.

    Source conflict reported rather than resolved by averaging: how the new year's start is defined. Smithsonian Magazine says the holiday marks "the first full moon of the lunar calendar"; the National Museum of Asian Art says it "begins with the first new moon of a lunar calendar"; the Congressional Research Service says it "starts on the second new moon after the winter solstice." The new-moon accounts are preferred above because they agree with the Hong Kong Observatory's own description of how the calendar is built and with the Lantern Festival falling on the fifteenth day; the full-moon wording is not adopted. The two sources also differ on the Gregorian range, the CRS giving January 21 to February 19 and Smithsonian Magazine January 21 to February 20; the CRS figure is used.
