    National Stuffing Day falls on November 21, a few days before the one meal of the year at which most American households make stuffing at all. No organization claims to have created the observance. What is well documented is the dish itself: its regional names, its best-known industrial version, and the federal food-safety guidance that governs it.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No founding body, proclamation, government page or news report designates a National Stuffing Day; the pages carrying the November 21 date are daily-holiday aggregators, and they cite nothing. This page does not supply a founder or a year to fill the gap.

    The dish is older than its names, and the names are harder to date than they look. Merriam-Webster puts the first known use of the noun "stuffing" in the 15th century and of "dressing" in the 14th — but in each case the dated sense is the general one, material used to fill something and the act of dressing. The dictionary attaches no date at all to the culinary meaning of either word.

    The regional split, at least, is on the record in an unlikely place. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service opens its own factsheet by conceding it: "Regionally in the U.S., it's called by various names: stuffing, filling, or dressing." The same page records how wide the ingredient list runs — grains, pasta, fruits, vegetables, shellfish, sausage, giblets and nuts, on top of the usual bread base.

    The best-documented chapter of American stuffing is industrial. On July 16, 1971, General Foods Corporation of White Plains, New York filed application 163,437 for an "Instant stuffing mix." U.S. Patent 3,870,803 issued on March 11, 1975, naming four inventors: Ruth M. Siems first, then Anthony C. Capossela, Jr., John F. Halligan and C. Robert Wyss. The claim was specific: a mix built from dried yeast-leavened corn bread crumb, or white bread crumb combined with whole wheat or corn bread crumb. The product was Stove Top. Siems, a home economist, died on November 13, 2005 at 74; the Associated Press obituary carried an Evansville, Indiana dateline, and NPR noted she was the first inventor listed on the patent.

    How to observe

    Read what USDA actually says, because it does not hedge. FSIS "does not recommend stuffing a whole turkey because it increases the risk of cross-contamination and takes longer to cook," and its standing advice is that "for optimal safety and uniform doneness, cook stuffing separately."

    If you stuff the bird anyway, the rules are precise. Do not combine the wet and dry ingredients until just before the stuffing goes in. Spoon it in loosely, about three-quarters of a cup per pound. Put the bird straight into an oven set no lower than 325 degrees Fahrenheit. Then take the temperature of the stuffing itself, not only the meat: the center has to reach 165 degrees Fahrenheit, and the bird should stand 20 minutes before the stuffing comes out. Never stuff a turkey the night before, never refrigerate uncooked stuffing, and never stuff a bird that will be deep-fried, because frying is too fast to carry heat to the center.

    For quantity, FSIS allows half a cup to a cup of prepared stuffing per pound of raw poultry, publishes a chart by bird weight, and suggests at least half a cup per person for stuffing baked in a casserole.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who created National Stuffing Day?

    Nobody that can be sourced. No founding organization, proclamation, government page or news report designates it, and the pages carrying the date are daily-holiday aggregators that cite nothing.

    Is it stuffing or dressing?

    Both, depending on where you live. USDA's own factsheet lists "stuffing, filling, or dressing" as regional names for the same dish and does not choose between them.

    Does the USDA really say not to stuff a turkey?

    Yes. FSIS states that it does not recommend stuffing a whole turkey, because doing so increases the risk of cross-contamination and takes longer to cook, and it recommends cooking stuffing separately. Stuffing the bird is not forbidden; it comes with specific handling rules.

    What temperature does stuffing need to reach?

    165 degrees Fahrenheit at the center, measured with a food thermometer — the same minimum as the turkey, and checked separately from it. If the meat is done and the stuffing is not, the bird goes back in the oven.

    Who invented Stove Top stuffing?

    Four General Foods inventors are named on the 1975 patent, with Ruth M. Siems listed first. She is usually credited alone; the patent credits her alongside Anthony C. Capossela, Jr., John F. Halligan and C. Robert Wyss.
