    National Candy Day is observed on November 4, and no organization appears to have put it there: the confectionery industry's own trade body does not promote it, and the date circulates on holiday calendars and in individual retailers' promotions without attribution. There was a genuine National Candy Day in American history, created by the candy trade and documented in contemporary newspapers — but it fell in October, and it is not this one.

    History

    No founder, founding year or sponsor can be documented for the November 4 observance. The National Confectioners Association's own website does not mention it; the single reference to the date anywhere on the association's domain is a 2023 item in its trade magazine about a member retailer running a November 4 promotion. Some confectionery trade press marks the same date as World Candy Day, also without naming an organizer. Mainstream outlets that cover it simply state the date.

    The documented one is older and sits on a different date. In the autumn of 1916, American candy manufacturers and confectioners agreed at a convention to set aside a day for their product. A full-page advertisement in The Ogden Standard of October 13, 1916 explains the arrangement in plain commercial terms: "in convention assembled, it was decided that October fourteenth should be the day. So it was ordered and decreed that that day should be called 'Nation-Wide Candy Day,' and that all the people should observe it." The same page's headline reads "Saturday, October 14 Is National Candy Day." A full-text search of the Library of Congress's digitized newspapers returns 53 pages carrying the phrase across October and November 1916, from Boise to Detroit to New Orleans, so the campaign was national rather than local. The second Saturday in October recurs in candy advertising into the 1920s.

    That observance did not survive the First World War. Sugar conservation under the U.S. Food Administration made a candy promotion awkward, and the day lapsed. By 1921 a Cleveland advertising executive, Herbert Birch Kingston, and a committee of eight local candy makers had recast it as Sweetest Day, first held on October 8, 1921, with 10,000 boxes distributed to orphanages, senior homes and charities. Sweetest Day is still on the calendar. November 4 has no documented connection to any of it.

    Timeline

    • 1847 — Oliver Chase, a Boston apothecary, builds a lozenge-cutting machine; he patents a sugar pulverizer in 1850 and a lozenge machine in 1857. The Smithsonian notes an 1847 patent cannot be confirmed.
    • 1864 — Thomas Mills & Brother of Philadelphia begins selling candy machinery; its 1874 catalog advertises a toy-candy machine producing 500 to 1,000 pounds a day.
    • October 14, 1916 — The first National Candy Day is observed on the second Saturday of October, the date set by the candy trade in convention.
    • ca. 1920 — Hershey installs the three-ton chocolate conche now held in the Smithsonian's collection.
    • October 8, 1921 — Cleveland holds the first Sweetest Day, after wartime sugar conservation ended the October Candy Day.
    • 2025 — U.S. confectionery retail sales reach a record $55 billion, according to the National Confectioners Association.

    How to observe

    Look at how candy stopped being handmade. Oliver Chase, an English-born Boston apothecary, built a machine in 1847 that stamped sheets of sugar paste into identical lozenges; he patented a sugar pulverizer in 1850 and a lozenge machine in 1857, and his firm became the New England Confectionery Company. The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History holds a three-ton chocolate conche from about 1920, used at the Hershey Chocolate Company, and its catalog record explains what conching does and why the machine is named after a shell.

    Cook from the period rather than reading about it: the Library of Congress has digitized Frye's Practical Candy Maker, a 96-page Chicago manual of hand-candy recipes from 1884, free to read page by page. Or search the 1916 newspapers yourself — Chronicling America is free and full-text searchable, and the advertisements announcing "the first National Candy Day" come up directly.

    Hammond's Candies in Denver, in business since 1920, runs free thirty-minute factory tours every half hour, Monday to Saturday, with booking a day ahead.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is National Candy Day?

    November 4, as the day is observed now. No organization has been found to have designated that date.

    Who started National Candy Day?

    Nobody who can be identified. The November 4 version has no documented founder, year or sponsor. A different National Candy Day was created by the American candy trade and first observed on Saturday, October 14, 1916, documented in newspaper advertising from the week itself.

    Is National Candy Day the same as World Candy Day?

    In practice, yes. Confectionery trade press places World Candy Day on November 4 as well, and names no organizer for it either.

    How big is the U.S. candy industry?

    The National Confectioners Association reports a record $55 billion in confectionery retail sales for 2025, with chocolate at 51.7 percent of the category. Its economic-impact study counts 203,248 direct jobs, 57,602 of them in manufacturing. The Census Bureau's 2015 Annual Survey of Manufactures counted 58,242 confectionery manufacturing workers — within about one percent of the trade body's figure.

    What is America's best-selling candy?

    This one has no honest answer from a source worth citing: neither a trade body nor a government agency publishes a brand-level sales ranking.
