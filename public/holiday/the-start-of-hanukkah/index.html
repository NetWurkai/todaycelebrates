    Celebration

    Hanukkah is an eight-night festival that begins on 25 Kislev and marks the rededication of the Second Temple in Jerusalem after the Maccabean revolt. A day in the Hebrew calendar begins at sundown, so the first candle is lit on the evening the festival opens — the date listed here — while 25 Kislev itself is the Gregorian day that follows. It was for most of its history a minor holiday; its prominence in American life is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century development, and its nearness to December 25 has a good deal to do with that.

    History

    The name means dedication, and the event behind it is dated with unusual precision in its earliest source. 1 Maccabees puts the rededication of the altar on the twenty-fifth of Kislev "in the year one hundred and forty-eight" of the Seleucid era, and records that "for eight days they celebrated the dedication of the altar." Converting that Seleucid year is where sources part company: most scholarship gives 164 BCE, including Britannica and the Posen Library's guide to the period, while two Smithsonian object records give 165 BCE. This page follows 164 and reports the disagreement rather than splitting it.

    Antiochus IV Epiphanes, the Hellenistic Syrian king, invaded Judaea and desecrated the Second Temple by dedicating it to Zeus; the revolt against him was led by the priest Mattathias and his son Judas Maccabeus, whose family founded the Hasmonean dynasty. The Posen Library dates the raid on the Temple to 169 BCE and the cult of Zeus Olympios there to 167 BCE, while noting that "the differences among our sources make it difficult to determine exactly what happened."

    Now the part most retellings leave out. The oil — one day's worth burning for eight — is not in 1 Maccabees, not in 2 Maccabees, and not in Josephus, who calls the festival the Festival of Lights and explains the name not by a miracle but by a liberty "beyond our wildest hopes." The cruse of oil first appears in the Babylonian Talmud at Shabbat 21b, compiled roughly six centuries later; Britannica calls it "a new twist" supplied by the rabbinic authorities, and the Jewish Theological Seminary separates the two strands explicitly. 2 Maccabees gives its own reason for the eight days: the people "celebrated joyfully for eight days as on the feast of Booths," having spent Sukkot that year hiding in caves.

    Its American scale is recent. The Library of Congress notes that "it was only in the later 19th century that it became a widely observed Jewish holiday," and Britannica, calling it "originally a minor holiday," attributes its more lavish modern celebration to its proximity to December 25.

    Timeline

    • 167 BCE — Antiochus IV Epiphanes introduces the cult of Zeus Olympios on the site of the Jerusalem Temple, two years after raiding it.
    • 164 BCE — Judas Maccabeus and his followers rededicate the Temple on 25 Kislev; 1 Maccabees dates it to the year 148 of the Seleucid era and records an eight-day celebration. Some sources, including two Smithsonian records, give 165 BCE.
    • c. 450–550 CE — The Babylonian Talmud is compiled. At Shabbat 21b it supplies the story of the single cruse of oil that burned for eight days, which appears in none of the earlier accounts.
    • 1979 — The first National Menorah is lit in Lafayette Park in Washington, with President Carter taking part; it moves to the Ellipse, its present location, some years later.
    • October 22, 1996 — The Postal Service issues the first United States Hanukkah stamp, a 32-cent joint issue with the Israeli Postal Authority and the first in its Holiday Celebrations series.
    • December 10, 2001 — The first Hanukkah lamp is lit in the White House residence, a hundred-year-old menorah borrowed from the Jewish Museum in New York.

    How to observe

    The lamp is the observance. A hanukkiah carries nine branches: eight for the nights, plus a holder set higher for the shammash, the "servant" candle, which exists because the Hanukkah lights themselves may not be used to kindle anything else. One candle the first night and one more each night after is Beit Hillel's position, which prevailed over Beit Shammai's, who began with eight and counted down. Shabbat 21b, which records both, also directs that the lamp be set at the entrance to the house on the outside, or at a window facing the street if you live upstairs — the window is what modern practice settled on.

    Fried food follows the oil: latkes and sufganiyot. The potato latke is a latecomer. Smithsonian Folklife records cheese latkes in central and southern Europe and buckwheat or rye flour in eastern Europe, the potato not entering the eastern European Jewish diet until the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

    The dreidel comes with a better story than the one most of us were told. Its four letters are traditionally read as an acronym for nes gadol haya sham, "a great miracle happened there." A Library of Congress folklorist traces the top itself to the European teetotum, borrowed probably in eighteenth-century German-speaking Europe, whose sides carried gambling abbreviations for all, half, nothing and put. On that account the Hebrew acronym is "just a later rationalization," and the familiar tale that dreidels disguised Torah study from the Greeks is "entirely ahistorical."

    For something to attend, the National Menorah is lit each December on the Ellipse in Washington under a National Park Service permit held by American Friends of Lubavitch. For something to read, the Library of Congress's Chronicling America holds digitized American Jewish newspapers, and The Jewish Cookery Book of 1871, believed to be the earliest Jewish cookbook issued in America, is online in full.

    Frequently asked questions

    When does Hanukkah start?

    On 25 Kislev, which moves against the Gregorian calendar and may fall any time from late November to late December. Because a day in the Hebrew calendar begins at sundown, the first candle is lit on the evening the festival opens, which is the date in the table on this page; 25 Kislev itself, the first full day, is the following Gregorian date. For 2026, Hebcal puts the start at sundown on Friday, December 4, with 25 Kislev 5787 falling on Saturday, December 5.

    Why does Hanukkah last eight days?

    Three answers, depending on the source. 1 Maccabees says the rededication was celebrated for eight days and the anniversary prescribed for eight. 2 Maccabees says the people kept eight days "as on the feast of Booths," having missed Sukkot while in hiding. The Talmud, centuries later, says one day's oil burned for eight. The first two are the earlier texts.

    Is the miracle of the oil in the Book of Maccabees?

    No. Neither 1 Maccabees 4 nor 2 Maccabees 10 mentions oil lasting eight days, and neither does Josephus, who describes the priests lighting the lamps and explains the festival's name by the unexpected recovery of liberty. The miracle first appears in the Babylonian Talmud at Shabbat 21b, roughly six centuries later.

    Is Hanukkah a federal holiday in the United States?

    No. Federal offices, banks and post offices operate normally. Federal involvement is ceremonial: the Postal Service has issued nine Hanukkah stamps since 1996, a Hanukkah lamp has been lit in the White House residence since 2001, and the National Park Service permits the National Menorah on the Ellipse each December.

    Is Hanukkah the Jewish Christmas?

    No, though the influence runs in that direction. The Library of Congress dates its emergence as a widely observed holiday to the later nineteenth century, and Britannica attributes its more lavish modern celebration to its nearness to December 25, naming gift-giving and home decoration as customs adopted from Christmas traditions.

    Source conflict reported rather than averaged: the year of the rededication. The primary datum is 1 Maccabees' Seleucid-era "year one hundred and forty-eight," not a BCE year at all. Of the derived dates, 164 BCE is given by Britannica in three separate articles, by Carol Bakhos's Posen Library guide published by Yale University Press, and by the New American Bible's footnote to John 10:22; 165 BCE is given by the Posen Library's own editorial gloss on the Josephus passage and by two Smithsonian object records. This page prints 164 BCE, names the disagreement, and does not assert a reason for it, because no source consulted explains the split.

    Second conflict: the dreidel. Britannica and the Postal Service present the nes gadol haya sham acronym as the meaning of the four letters; the Library of Congress's folklife blog traces the letters to German gambling abbreviations and calls the acronym a later rationalization, with the Torah-study story "entirely ahistorical." The Library of Congress article is the more specific and more rigorous on origins and is preferred on that point; the acronym is still given as the traditional reading, because that is what it is.

    Third conflict: where the first National Menorah was lit in 1979. The White House Historical Association and NPR say Lafayette Park; an Obama-era White House blog post says the Ellipse. Smithsonian Magazine's account of a later move to the Ellipse reconciles them in favor of Lafayette Park, and this page says Lafayette Park in 1979 rather than claiming the Ellipse since 1979.

    Fourth conflict: the first White House menorah lighting. NPR calls President Clinton in 1993 the first to light a menorah in the people's house; the Bush White House archive says 2001 was the first year a menorah was lit in the White House residence. These are reconcilable, since the Oval Office is in the West Wing rather than the Executive Residence, and the page uses the residence formulation.

    Omitted for want of an acceptable source: any figure for the share of American Jews who light Hanukkah candles. Pew has never asked the question — the word does not appear in the topline of either its 2013 or its 2020 survey of Jewish Americans — so any such statistic in circulation is unsourced, and none is printed here. Also omitted: the origin or history of Hanukkah gelt; any claim that work is permitted on Hanukkah, which no consulted source states; the ISBN and page count of Hanukkah in America, whose publisher page could not be read; the name of whoever lights the National Menorah in a given year; and any standalone congressional resolution recognizing Hanukkah itself, since only H.Res. 615 of 2005 could be verified and it addresses Hanukkah, Ramadan and Kwanzaa together on First Amendment grounds.

    Less than fully confident, flagged deliberately: "the later nineteenth century" for Hanukkah's emergence as a widely observed holiday rests on a single Library of Congress blog sentence, and Britannica's "originally a minor holiday" is the only citable support for the minor-holiday framing. Both are reputable but neither is a dedicated scholarly treatment, and the page does not build anything further on them.

    One source is used outside the site's usual allow-list and is named here deliberately: the White House Historical Association, for the sequence of White House menorahs and the 1993 Oval Office lighting. It is an author-attributed, footnoted institutional history, and every claim drawn from it that matters is corroborated by the George W. Bush White House archive or the Obama White House archive.
