    Good Grief Day falls on November 26, which is the birthday of Charles M. Schulz, who drew Peanuts for very nearly fifty years. The name borrows Charlie Brown's standard exclamation — Merriam-Webster lists "good grief" as an informal phrase "used to express surprise or annoyance." Who attached it to this date, and when, cannot be established from any reliable source.

    History

    The origin of this observance is not well documented. No museum, publisher, estate or news outlet claims it; the day appears only on calendar aggregators, none of which names a founder or a first year, and none of which explains the date. The date does line up with Schulz's birth, and that much is sourced, though no source states the birthday as the reason for the day.

    The Charles M. Schulz Museum and Research Center records that he was born in Minneapolis on November 26, 1922, at 919 Chicago Avenue South. Two days later an uncle nicknamed him Sparky, after Spark Plug, the horse in the comic strip Barney Google; the name stuck for the rest of his life. His first published drawing appeared in 1937 in Ripley's syndicated Believe It or Not feature — a picture of the family dog, Spike, signed "Sparky," sent in by his father. He sold a weekly panel called Li'l Folks to the St. Paul Pioneer Press, and in 1950 sold the feature to United Feature Syndicate, which renamed it. By the museum's account Schulz did not like the new title.

    The first Peanuts strip ran on October 2, 1950, in seven newspapers, with Charlie Brown, Shermy and Patty. What followed was steady rather than sudden: 355 American and 40 foreign papers by 1958; two Reuben Awards from the National Cartoonists Society by 1965, the year A Charlie Brown Christmas won both a Peabody and an Emmy; a place in the Guinness Book of World Records when the strip was sold to its 2,000th newspaper in 1984. When Schulz announced his retirement in 1999, Peanuts was syndicated in more than 2,600 newspapers worldwide and the book collections had been translated into more than 25 languages.

    He died in his sleep on the night of Saturday, February 12, 2000, of complications from colon cancer. The last original Peanuts strip ran the next morning, hours later, in the Sunday papers. It was his 17,897th. The museum opened in Santa Rosa, California, in 2002, and by its own count the strip still circulates in about 1,700 newspapers.

    Timeline

    • November 26, 1922 — Charles M. Schulz is born at home in Minneapolis, Minnesota; an uncle nicknames him Sparky two days later.
    • 1937 — His first published drawing, of the family dog Spike, appears in Ripley's syndicated Believe It or Not.
    • October 2, 1950 — The first Peanuts strip runs in seven newspapers, with Charlie Brown, Shermy and Patty, after United Feature Syndicate renames Schulz's Li'l Folks.
    • 1965 — A Charlie Brown Christmas wins a Peabody Award and an Emmy; by this year the National Cartoonists Society has given Schulz two Reuben Awards.
    • 1984 — Peanuts enters the Guinness Book of World Records on being sold to its 2,000th newspaper.
    • February 12–13, 2000 — Schulz dies of complications from colon cancer; the final original strip, his 17,897th, runs the following morning.

    How to observe

    Read the first strip and the last one together. The October 2, 1950 strip has a cast of three — Charlie Brown, Shermy and Patty — and reads nothing like the thing Peanuts became; the one printed on February 13, 2000 is the last he drew. Set side by side they cover fifty years in two minutes.

    Work through the museum's own timeline rather than a summary of it. The Charles M. Schulz Museum publishes a dated chronology that quotes him directly and includes the small things — the address he was born at, the Ripley's drawing, the panels nobody remembers — which is where the interesting material actually lives.

    Watch A Charlie Brown Christmas, which won a Peabody and an Emmy in 1965 and is the reason a lot of people know the strip at all.

    Call it Li'l Folks once, for accuracy. That was Schulz's title for the feature; Peanuts was the syndicate's, and he did not care for it.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is Good Grief Day?

    November 26, every year.

    Who started Good Grief Day?

    No one that can be documented. The day appears only on calendar aggregator sites, none of which names a founder, a first year or an organization behind it. The origin of this observance is not well documented.

    Why November 26?

    It is the day Charles M. Schulz was born, in Minneapolis in 1922. That is the only evident connection, and no reliable source actually states it as the reason for the date.

    Was Peanuts always called Peanuts?

    No. Schulz sold the feature as Li'l Folks, which had run as a weekly panel in the St. Paul Pioneer Press. United Feature Syndicate renamed it when it bought the strip in 1950, and by the Schulz museum's account he never liked the title.

    How many Peanuts strips did Schulz draw?

    17,897. The first ran on October 2, 1950, in seven newspapers; the last original strip ran on February 13, 2000, the morning after he died.
