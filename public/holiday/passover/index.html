    Passover

    Passover begins on 15 Nisan and retells the Exodus from Egypt at a ritual meal, the seder, held in the home rather than the synagogue. Because a day in the Hebrew calendar begins at sundown, the first seder falls on the evening before the Gregorian date of the first day — a distinction worth holding on to when reading any calendar, including this one. It runs seven days in Israel and for most Reform Jews and eight in traditional Diaspora practice, and it is the most widely kept Jewish observance in the United States: six in ten American Jews told Pew they had held or attended a seder in the previous year.

    History

    The instructions are in Exodus 12, and they are specific. A lamb for each household, taken on the tenth day of the month; its blood "on the two side-posts and on the lintel"; the flesh eaten that night "roast with fire, and unleavened bread; with bitter herbs," in haste, "with your loins girded, your shoes on your feet, and your staff in your hand." The name is explained inside the text: "when I see the blood" — "I will pass over you, and there shall no plague be upon you." Passover renders the Hebrew pesach, from the verb for passing over. The same chapter sets the seven days of unleavened bread and the evening-to-evening boundary that still governs the holiday, "the fourteenth day of the month at even" until "the one and twentieth day of the month at even." Quotations here follow the 1917 Jewish Publication Society translation.

    While the Temple stood, Passover was one of three festivals that required a pilgrimage to Jerusalem; Exodus 23 commands that "three times in the year all thy males shall appear before the Lord GOD." The destruction of the Second Temple ended the sacrifice, and what replaced it was the seder. Robert Harris of the Jewish Theological Seminary calls the change a "seismic shift": in place of the now impossible Temple ritual the rabbis adopted the form of the Roman symposium, a meal built around questions and discourse — and he adds that they could have told Jews to perform the sacrifice at home, and chose not to.

    The seder's furniture is older than most people assume. Mishnah Pesachim 10, compiled around 200 CE, already has the four cups of wine, the reclining that "even the poorest of Jews" should not eat without, the child's question — "Why is this night different from all other nights?" — the bitter herbs, and the line that carries the whole evening: "In each and every generation a person must view himself as though he personally left Egypt." The same passage notes that eating charoset "is not a mitzva but merely a custom." The Haggadah, which scripts the evening, is attested in a tenth-century prayer book compiled by Rabbi Saadia Gaon; the Library of Congress calls it the most widely read classic text in the Jewish tradition after the Bible, and holds more than three thousand printed editions.

    Timeline

    • c. 200 CE — The Mishnah, at Pesachim 10, records the four cups of wine, the reclining, the child's question and the bitter herbs, and notes that charoset is a custom rather than a commandment.
    • 10th century — The oldest complete version of the Haggadah appears in a prayer book compiled by Rabbi Saadia Gaon.
    • 1478 — Joel ben Simeon, an itinerant scribe from Germany, writes what is now the Library of Congress's Washington Haggadah; the Library published a facsimile of it in 1993.
    • 1846 — The Breslau rabbinical conference judges the second days of festivals to "have no longer any significance for our time," the step behind the Reform movement's seven-day Passover.
    • April 4, 1969 — The first Freedom Seder is held in the basement of Lincoln Temple in Washington, DC, on the first anniversary of Dr. King's assassination; about 800 people attend.
    • April 9, 2009 — President Obama hosts a seder in the Old Family Dining Room, which he later called "the first-ever White House Seder"; eight followed over his two terms.

    How to observe

    Chametz — leaven — is what the holiday turns on practically. The prohibited grains are wheat, rye, barley, oats and spelt, and the prohibition runs the whole festival, while eating matzah is an obligation only at the seder. Commercial matzah production runs on an eighteen-minute limit for how long dough may sit before baking, after which the lines are stopped and cleaned. Ashkenazi practice additionally avoids kitniyot, foods resembling the forbidden grains, including rice; Mediterranean and Middle Eastern traditions generally do not.

    The seder plate is where meaning gets attached to food: parsley dipped in salt water for the tears of slavery, an egg for the rebirth of spring, bitter herbs for bitterness, charoset as custom rather than commandment, and the broken matzah whose larger piece is hidden and eaten later as the afikoman. Many American plates now also carry an orange, added as a symbol of feminism. The meal ends with "Next year in Jerusalem."

    The most distinctly American thing about Passover is what Americans have used it to say. On April 4, 1969, the first anniversary of Dr. King's assassination, Arthur Waskow wrote a new Haggadah and about 800 people — Black and white, Jewish and Christian — gathered in the basement of Lincoln Temple, a Black church in Washington, for what became the Freedom Seder, weaving the liberation of the ancient Hebrews together with the liberation struggles of Black America.

    For primary sources this is one of the better-served holidays online. The Library of Congress has digitized the Washington Haggadah, written in 1478 by the itinerant scribe Joel ben Simeon, and holds Abraham Berman's collection of 600 more. The National Library of Israel's 8,500 is the largest such collection in the world. All of it is free to read.

    Frequently asked questions

    When is the first seder?

    On the evening before the first day of Passover. A day in the Hebrew calendar begins at sundown, so 15 Nisan starts the night before the Gregorian date calendars print for it. For 2027, Hebcal puts the start at sundown on Wednesday, April 21, with the first full day, 15 Nisan 5787, on Thursday, April 22 — the date in the table on this page. A second seder follows the next night wherever eight days are kept.

    Is Passover seven days or eight?

    Seven in Israel and for most Reform Jews, eight in traditional Diaspora practice. The difference is a survival. Before the Hebrew calendar was computed mathematically, the new month was fixed by witnesses sighting the crescent moon in Jerusalem, and distant communities could not be sure which of two days it had begun on, so they kept each festival day twice. The calculation removed the original reason but the custom stayed. Reform Judaism traces its one-day standard to the 1846 Breslau rabbinical conference.

    Is Passover a federal holiday in the United States?

    No. Federal offices, banks and post offices operate normally, and there is no observed day off. The Postal Service's Jewish-holiday stamp series is Hanukkah, now in its ninth issue; no Passover stamp appears in its current or announced programs. The first seder hosted by a sitting president at the White House was in 2009.

    How many American Jews hold a seder?

    Six in ten, in Pew's survey fielded from November 2019 to June 2020 — the most widely practiced ritual that survey measured, ahead of fasting on Yom Kippur at 46 percent. Pew's earlier 2013 survey put it at 70 percent.

    Why matzah?

    Unleavened bread recalls a departure too hurried for dough to rise. Exodus 12 requires seven days of it and the removal of leaven from the house. In practice the two rules have different reach: the ban on leaven applies to the whole festival, while the obligation to eat matzah applies only at the seder.

    Source conflict reported rather than averaged: seder attendance. Pew's 2013 survey found 70 percent and its 2020 survey six in ten. This is the same organization in two survey waves, not a contradiction, and the page uses the more recent figure with its survey window named, citing the 2013 number separately and labeled.

    Second conflict: when Passover ends, on 21 or 22 Nisan. Britannica's video page says "the 21st or 22nd"; Hebcal gives 15 to 22 Nisan for the Diaspora and 15 to 21 Nisan for Israel. These agree once the Israel and Diaspora schedules are separated, and Hebcal, as a calendar computation that publishes both schedules explicitly, is treated as the more primary for date mechanics.

    Third conflict: the first White House seder. The President's own 2016 statement in the Compilation of Presidential Documents says "the first-ever White House Seder in 2009." An institutional history records a 1993 seder held by Clinton staff in the Indian Treaty Room of the Eisenhower Executive Office Building, across the street from the White House and without the President present. That refines rather than refutes the presidential statement, and this page uses the precise form: the first seder hosted by a sitting president at the White House. The 1993 staff event is not asserted, because it could not be confirmed from a source on the site's allow-list.

    Omitted for want of an acceptable source: the claim that the Haggadah is the most-printed Jewish book, which no library or museum source consulted supports — the Library of Congress's "most widely read classic text in the Jewish tradition" is used instead; the convention that the youngest child asks the Four Questions; a definition of the cup of Elijah; the yellow cap on kosher-for-Passover Coca-Cola, and Manischewitz's founding year, founder or city; the Hebrew term shalosh regalim; publication of the Freedom Seder Haggadah in Ramparts; and any statement about historians' views on the historicity of the Exodus or divergence between archaeology and the biblical narrative, which would need a dedicated pass at books and journals rather than the sources reached here. The page therefore presents Exodus 12 as what the text prescribes, not as an account of events.

    Less than fully confident, flagged deliberately: the afikoman. The practice of hiding the larger broken piece and eating it later is described in a first-person Smithsonian Folklife essay rather than in a reference definition, so the page states it in one clause and builds nothing on it. Also flagged: the statement that no Passover stamp exists is a negative finding from a search of the Postal Service's own newsroom and its announced 2026 program, and is worded as such rather than as a historical absolute.
