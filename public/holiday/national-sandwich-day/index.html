    National Sandwich Day falls on November 3 and honors the most unremarkable food in the American lunch rotation: a filling between two pieces of bread, named for an eighteenth-century British politician who almost certainly did not invent it. The observance has no documented founder, and the explanation usually given for its date does not survive a look at the record.

    History

    The sandwich takes its name from John Montagu, the 4th Earl of Sandwich, a British politician best known in his own time as First Lord of the Admiralty. The eating itself was nothing new. Britannica's account of the food notes that this way of consuming bread and meat "must be as old as meat and bread"; what the earl supplied was a name, and with it a fashion. Once his title was attached, serving sandwiches became stylish on the European continent, and the English word was taken into French.

    The story everyone tells is the gaming table: that during a long session of play the earl had sliced meat and bread brought to him so he would not have to leave the game. It comes from a contemporary French travel book, and Britannica is skeptical of it twice over. Its biography of Montagu calls the version tied to a specific 1762 incident — twenty-four hours at the table without other food — "widely thought to be apocryphal." Its article on the food offers duller alternatives as the likelier ones: that he ate them at his desk while working, or that London society simply noticed him ordering them.

    Montagu was an administrator, not a wit. Britannica gives his birth as November 13, 1718. He succeeded his grandfather as earl in 1729, held the Admiralty across more than one spell, served as postmaster general and as secretary of state for the northern department, and prosecuted John Wilkes in 1763, earning the lasting nickname "Jemmy Twitcher." His promotion of exploration led Captain James Cook to name the Sandwich Islands — Hawaii — after him in 1778. He died in London on April 30, 1792.

    The observance is another matter. The origin of National Sandwich Day is not well documented: no founding organization, proclamation, or first observance can be sourced for it. The explanation in general circulation is that November 3 was the earl's birthday, but the most reputable source available gives November 13, 1718, and no primary record reachable here settles the difference. Calendar reform is the obvious suspect — Britain kept the Julian calendar until 1752, and eighteenth-century dates are often restated eleven days later — except that eleven days from November 3 lands on the 14th, not the 13th. Until someone can source it, the usual explanation stays an explanation.

    Timeline

    • 1718 — John Montagu, the future 4th Earl of Sandwich, is born; Britannica gives the date as November 13.
    • 1729 — He succeeds his grandfather, the 3rd earl, and becomes the 4th Earl of Sandwich.
    • 1762 — The year of the gaming-table anecdote recorded in a contemporary French travel book, the story Britannica calls widely thought to be apocryphal.
    • 18th century — The name "sandwich" is adopted for the food; serving them becomes fashionable on the European continent and the word passes into French.
    • 1778 — Captain James Cook names the Sandwich Islands, now Hawaii, after the earl.
    • 1792 — Montagu dies in London on April 30.

    How to observe

    Make the plainest possible version once: cold sliced meat between two pieces of bread, nothing toasted, no spread. That is all the earl is supposed to have eaten, and the distance between it and whatever you normally build is the history of the form in a single bite.

    Then read Britannica's two entries, on the earl and on the food, back to back. Both name the gaming-table anecdote and both decline to endorse it. Watching a good story get hedged twice by the same reference work is a short lesson in how food history is actually made.

    If you want something to cook, take the American contributions Britannica singles out — the club, chicken or turkey with bacon, lettuce, and tomato, and the Reuben, corned beef with Swiss cheese, sauerkraut, and Russian dressing, grilled on black bread — and make whichever of the two you have never made.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why is a sandwich called a sandwich?

    It is named for John Montagu, the 4th Earl of Sandwich. Britannica says the name was adopted for the food in the eighteenth century, and that the earl's title gave the preparation enough cachet to make it fashionable across Europe.

    Did the Earl of Sandwich invent the sandwich?

    No. Britannica notes that eating meat with bread "must be as old as meat and bread." The earl contributed the name, not the idea.

    Is National Sandwich Day on the Earl of Sandwich's birthday?

    That is the explanation usually given, but it does not hold up cleanly. Britannica dates his birth to November 13, 1718, not November 3, and nothing reachable here resolves the ten-day gap.

    Who started National Sandwich Day?

    No one traceable. The origin of this observance is not well documented, and no founding organization, proclamation, or first observance can be sourced for it.

    Why were the Hawaiian Islands once called the Sandwich Islands?

    Captain James Cook named them after the earl in 1778, in recognition of his support for naval exploration.
