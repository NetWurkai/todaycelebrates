    Guinness World Records Day is the record company's own annual invitation to break something: a day set aside for attempts staged in schools, offices and town squares, many of them adjudicated on the spot. Guinness World Records says it was "first held in 2004 to mark Guinness World Records becoming the best-selling copyright book of all time." The date is not fixed — the company sets it each year, and for 2026 its own page gives Thursday, November 19.

    History

    The book came first, and it came out of an argument nobody could settle. Guinness World Records' own account places it on "an unknown date in November 1951," when Sir Hugh Beaver (1890-1967) was out shooting on Slob Hill, by the river Slaney in County Wexford. Some golden plovers were missed by the party, and the question arose of which was the fastest game bird in Europe. No reference book on any shelf could answer it. The company's eventual answer, years later, was the red-breasted merganser, Mergus serrator.

    On 12 September 1954, Norris and Ross McWhirter — twins then running a fact-finding agency in London — were invited to discuss the proposition; the runner Chris Chataway had recommended them. The first copy of the Guinness Book of Records was completed on 30 June 1955, with a publication date of 3 October 1955. Norris McWhirter remained editor-in-chief until 1986, after the death of his twin brother. The book was later renamed Guinness World Records, which the company places in the new millennium without giving a year.

    In 1974 the book "became the biggest-selling copyright book in history," and in 2019 the company reported that more than 143 million editions had been sold worldwide since 1954. Guinness World Records Day grew directly out of that distinction: first held in 2004, it is now, in the company's words, "a much-anticipated annual celebration that has inspired some iconic record-breaking achievements."

    How to observe

    Start with the rules, not the training. Guinness World Records' own site is where record titles and their requirements are published, and an attempt is judged against those requirements rather than against how impressive it looked — which means the reading has to come first. An attempt documented the wrong way cannot be verified afterwards, however well it went.

    Choosing an existing title is simpler than proposing a new one. Groups tend to do best with records built for groups: the largest number of people doing one simple thing at once, in one place, for a set length of time. Those are the attempts a single coordinated day is designed around, which is why so many of them happen in schools and offices.

    If you have no intention of attempting anything, the day is a good excuse to go back to the question that started the whole enterprise. Which is the fastest game bird in Europe? In 1951 no book in Britain or Ireland could say, and that gap on the shelf is the entire reason the Guinness Book of Records exists. Guinness's answer, when it came, was a duck.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Guinness World Records Day on the same date every year?

    No. Guinness World Records sets the date and it moves. The company's own GWR Day page gives Thursday, November 19 for 2026, which is the third Thursday of the month. If you are planning an attempt, take the date from Guinness rather than from a calendar listing — including this one.

    When did Guinness World Records Day start?

    2004, according to the company. Its own page says the day was "first held in 2004 to mark Guinness World Records becoming the best-selling copyright book of all time."

    How did the Guinness Book of Records begin?

    With an argument. Guinness says that on an unknown date in November 1951, Sir Hugh Beaver was out shooting in County Wexford when the party missed some golden plovers and began debating which was Europe's fastest game bird. Nothing on the shelves could answer it. The first edition was published on 3 October 1955.

    Who were the McWhirter twins?

    Norris and Ross McWhirter ran a fact-finding agency in London and were brought in to compile the book after Chris Chataway recommended them, at a meeting the company dates to 12 September 1954. Norris stayed on as editor-in-chief until 1986, after his brother's death.

    How many copies has the book sold?

    Guinness reported in 2019 that more than 143 million editions had been sold worldwide since 1954. The book has held the record for best-selling copyright book since 1974.
