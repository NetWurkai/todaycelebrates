    The International Day for Tolerance falls on November 16, and it has two founding documents rather than one: UNESCO proclaimed the day in 1995, and the United Nations General Assembly invited member states to observe it the year after. The date was not chosen at random. November 16 is the anniversary of UNESCO's own constitution, adopted on that day in 1945, and the declaration that created the observance was adopted exactly fifty years later.

    History

    Two documents made this day, and the difference between them is worth keeping straight.

    The first is UNESCO's Declaration of Principles on Tolerance, adopted in Paris on November 16, 1995, on the closing day of the organization's twenty-eighth General Conference, which had been sitting since October 25. Article 6 of the declaration is headed "International Day for Tolerance," and its operative line reads: "we solemnly proclaim 16 November the annual International Day for Tolerance." The declaration's preamble explains the date by recalling that the preamble to UNESCO's own constitution was "adopted on 16 November 1945" — so the proclamation landed on the organization's fiftieth anniversary, to the day.

    The second is United Nations General Assembly resolution 51/95, adopted in 1996 under the title "Follow-Up to the United Nations Year for Tolerance." The UN's own account of what that resolution did is carefully worded: "In 1996, the UN General Assembly (by resolution 51/95) invited UN Member States to observe the International Day for Tolerance on 16 November, with activities directed towards both educational establishments and the wider public." Invited, not proclaimed. UNESCO created the day; the General Assembly put it in front of every member state, with an audience named.

    Both followed the United Nations Year for Tolerance, which the General Assembly proclaimed in 1993 at UNESCO's initiative and which ran through 1995. UNESCO added a prize the same year: the UNESCO-Madanjeet Singh Prize for the Promotion of Tolerance and Non-Violence, created to mark the Year for Tolerance and the 125th anniversary of the birth of Mahatma Gandhi, and awarded every two years on November 16.

    Timeline

    • November 16, 1945 — The preamble to UNESCO's constitution is adopted. The Declaration of Principles on Tolerance later cites that date, which is why the observance falls on November 16.
    • 1993 — The UN General Assembly proclaims 1995 the United Nations Year for Tolerance, at UNESCO's initiative.
    • October 25 – November 16, 1995 — UNESCO's twenty-eighth General Conference meets in Paris.
    • November 16, 1995 — Member states adopt the Declaration of Principles on Tolerance. Article 6 proclaims November 16 the annual International Day for Tolerance, on UNESCO's fiftieth anniversary.
    • 1995 — UNESCO creates the UNESCO-Madanjeet Singh Prize for the Promotion of Tolerance and Non-Violence, awarded every two years on November 16.
    • 1996 — General Assembly resolution 51/95, "Follow-Up to the United Nations Year for Tolerance," invites member states to observe the day.

    How to observe

    Read the declaration. UNESCO publishes the full text and it is short. Article 6, the clause that created this observance, sits at the end of a list of principles rather than at the top, which says something about the order of priorities: the day was the follow-through on a statement of position, not the point of it.

    If you work in a school, you are the intended audience. The General Assembly's 1996 resolution asked for activities "directed towards both educational establishments and the wider public," which makes November 16 one of the few international observances with a venue written into its founding text.

    Follow the prize rather than the day. The UNESCO-Madanjeet Singh Prize is awarded on November 16 every second year, and its laureates are particular organizations doing particular work — the 2020 award went to Centre Résolution Conflits in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Reading down the list of winners is a more concrete account of what the day is for than any slogan.

    Frequently asked questions

    Who created the International Day for Tolerance?

    UNESCO did, in Article 6 of its Declaration of Principles on Tolerance, adopted November 16, 1995. The UN General Assembly followed in 1996 with resolution 51/95, which invited member states to observe it.

    Why is it held on November 16?

    It is the anniversary of UNESCO's constitution, whose preamble was adopted on November 16, 1945. The declaration that proclaimed the day was adopted on the same date fifty years later.

    Is it a public holiday in the United States?

    No. Like other United Nations observances it carries no official status in American law, and offices, banks, schools and post offices operate normally.

    Is there a prize attached to the day?

    Yes. The UNESCO-Madanjeet Singh Prize for the Promotion of Tolerance and Non-Violence was created in 1995 and is awarded every two years on November 16. The 2020 award went to Centre Résolution Conflits in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

    Did the General Assembly proclaim the day?

    No, and the UN's own wording is specific: resolution 51/95 invited member states to observe it. The proclamation was UNESCO's, a year earlier.
