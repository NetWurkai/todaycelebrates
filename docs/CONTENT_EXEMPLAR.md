# Content Exemplar — Halloween

**This is the reference. Every content draft matches it on structure, voice, and
sourcing.** Read it before writing. It is deliberately a hard case: Halloween's
popular history is full of confident claims that thin out when you look for a
source, which is exactly the failure mode this spec exists to prevent.

## What makes this the exemplar

1. **It names what it cannot prove.** The Samhain-to-Halloween line is usually
   told as direct descent. The sourced version is more layered, and the draft
   says that rather than picking the tidier story.
2. **Specifics over atmosphere.** "William W. Kolb, a Newark candymaker, in 1908"
   instead of "Halloween treats have a long history."
3. **The observe section is actionable.** Real things a reader can do, including
   one they would not have thought of.
4. **No boilerplate.** Compare the current live copy: *"Halloween, observed on
   October 31, celebrates…"* — that sentence fits any holiday, which is why it
   is worth nothing.
5. **The FAQ answers real queries**, including the boring, high-volume ones
   ("is it a federal holiday") that people actually search.

## Draft

### Opening

Halloween falls on October 31 and is, by most measures, the most widely observed
non-federal holiday in the United States. It is also one of the least
historically tidy: what Americans do on October 31 is a composite of a Gaelic
harvest festival, a Catholic feast, Scottish and Irish neighborhood customs,
and a twentieth-century American candy industry that found the whole thing
extremely useful.

### History

The evening takes its name from the Christian calendar. In the eighth century,
Pope Gregory III designated November 1 as a day to honor the saints; Christian
missionaries later placed All Souls' Day on November 2. The night before
November 1 became All Hallows' Eve, and then Halloween.

Older than the name is Samhain — a Gaelic festival marking the end of summer and
the harvest, pronounced *SAH-win*. Samhain is almost always described as
Halloween's ancestor, and it is a genuine precursor. But the line is not as
direct as it is usually told: the specific things Americans do on October 31
arrived by way of centuries of Christian observance and then nineteenth-century
Irish and Scottish immigration, not as an unbroken inheritance from pre-Christian
Ireland. The jack-o'-lantern, for instance, began in Ireland as a carved turnip.
Pumpkins — native to North America, larger, and far easier to hollow out — were
a New World substitution.

Going door to door has two sourced precursors. **Souling**, a medieval practice,
sent the poor and especially children from house to house on All Souls' Day
offering prayers for the dead in exchange for food or money. **Guising**, a
Scottish custom, was its secular descendant: a costume, a performance, a small
payment. The phrase "trick or treat" itself is recent — first recorded in 1927 —
and the practice spread through the United States during the 1920s. In the early
1930s, community groups actively promoted it as a way to redirect Halloween
pranking, which had grown destructive enough to worry municipalities.

What made it universal was mass culture and sugar. A 1951 *Peanuts* strip had
Charlie Brown saying "tricks or treats"; Disney released a Donald Duck short
called *Trick or Treat* in 1952. The end of wartime sugar rationing did the rest,
and the custom became general through the 1950s.

### Timeline

- **43 CE** — Rome conquers the British Isles. The harvest festival of Pomona,
  goddess of fruit trees, is later connected to bobbing for apples.
- **8th century** — Pope Gregory III designates November 1 to honor the saints.
  The evening before becomes All Hallows' Eve.
- **Middle Ages** — Souling: the poor go door to door on All Souls' Day, offering
  prayers for the dead in return for food or money.
- **1880s–1908** — The American Halloween candy industry takes shape. A
  candymaker at Philadelphia's Wunderle Candy Company is credited with candy
  corn in the 1880s; the Goelitz Company takes it mass-market in 1898. William W.
  Kolb, a Newark candymaker, is credited with the candy apple in 1908.
- **1927** — "Trick or treat" is recorded in print for the first time.
- **1951–1952** — *Peanuts* and Disney put the phrase in front of a national
  audience; trick-or-treating becomes general over the decade that follows.

### How to observe

Carve a turnip instead of a pumpkin at least once. It is genuinely difficult,
which is the point — it explains immediately why the pumpkin won.

Check your municipality's trick-or-treat hours before the 31st. Many American
towns set official windows, and a surprising number schedule them on a different
evening entirely when October 31 falls awkwardly. Local government sites post
them; assuming October 31 at dusk is how families end up on empty streets.

If you want the unmediated version of Halloween's American history, the Library
of Congress's Chronicling America archive holds digitized newspapers back to the
1700s. Searching "Halloween" against your own town in the 1890s through 1920s
turns up the parties, the pranks, and the civic hand-wringing as they were
actually reported — including the vandalism that trick-or-treating was invented
to absorb.

### FAQ

**Is Halloween always October 31?**
Yes. Unlike Thanksgiving or Easter, the date is fixed. In 2026 it falls on a
Saturday.

**Is Halloween a federal holiday?**
No. Federal offices, banks, and post offices operate normally, and there is no
observed day off. It is among the most widely marked days in the country and has
no official status at all.

**Why do we say "trick or treat"?**
The phrase is first recorded in 1927 and spread with the custom through the 1920s
and 1930s. The "trick" was not rhetorical — Halloween pranking was serious enough
in the early 1930s that community groups promoted treat-giving specifically to
head it off.

**Why pumpkins instead of turnips?**
The jack-o'-lantern is an Irish tradition that used turnips. Pumpkins are native
to North America, far bigger, and much easier to carve, so Irish immigrants
switched.

**When did trick-or-treating actually become common?**
It spread in the 1920s, was promoted in the 1930s as an alternative to vandalism,
and became general in the 1950s after sugar rationing ended.
